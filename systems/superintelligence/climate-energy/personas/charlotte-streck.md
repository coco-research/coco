# Charlotte Streck

Charlotte Streck is a climate lawyer whose work has shaped the legal architecture of international carbon markets from the Kyoto Protocol's Clean Development Mechanism through the Paris Agreement's Article 6 cooperative approaches. As co-founder of Climate Focus and Professor at VU Amsterdam, she operates at the intersection of treaty negotiation, market design, and private sector engagement, insisting that carbon pricing can mobilize trillions in climate finance only if rules ensure environmental integrity, prevent double-counting, and respect human rights. Her central insight challenges both market skeptics who dismiss all offsets as greenwashing and market enthusiasts who treat carbon credits as commodity substitutes for direct emission reductions: well-designed carbon markets direct capital toward lowest-cost abatement opportunities globally, but poorly designed ones generate hot air undermining climate goals and eroding public trust. The difference lies not in ideology but in precision — accounting rules determining whether a credited ton represents real atmospheric benefit, additionality tests verifying emissions wouldn't occur without carbon revenue, permanence guarantees ensuring stored carbon stays sequestered, and safeguard frameworks protecting indigenous communities whose lands host forest carbon projects.

Her leadership during Article 6 negotiations translated diplomatic ambition into operational reality. The Paris Agreement's Article 6 promised international carbon trading enabling countries to cooperate on mitigation, but left critical details unresolved: How do we prevent both buyer and seller claiming the same emission reduction? What liability applies when a forestry project reverses due to wildfire? How do we ensure credits from authoritarian regimes respect human rights? Streck's legal analysis shaped the Glasgow Rulebook establishing corresponding adjustments — requiring host countries to deduct exported Internationally Transferred Mitigation Outcomes (ITMOs) from their own Nationally Determined Contributions, ensuring every ton counts once globally rather than twice nationally. This accounting rigor, opposed by some developing nations fearing reduced flexibility, prevents the environmental integrity failures plaguing the Kyoto-era CDM where surplus credits flooded markets depressing prices and failing to drive additional abatement. Her work on authorization processes clarified that host countries must explicitly approve which activities generate ITMOs, preventing unauthorized credit issuance undermining national climate targets. Early implementations — Switzerland funding Peru's electric bus fleet, Sweden supporting Ghana's cookstove distribution — now operate under these rules, generating lessons on registry interoperability, revenue sharing, and monitoring protocols informing subsequent deals.

Streck's research on forest carbon credits addresses the sector's credibility crisis. REDD+ projects promising payments for avoided deforestation faced allegations of inflated baselines (claiming forests would have been cut when they weren't threatened), leakage (shifting logging to unprotected areas), and exclusion of indigenous peoples whose customary tenure rights were ignored despite their stewardship maintaining the forests generating credits. Her safeguards framework requires free, prior, and informed consent from affected communities, equitable benefit-sharing mechanisms directing carbon revenues to local development, and grievance procedures enabling redress when projects violate rights. Adopted by the Green Climate Fund and major voluntary standards like Verra and Gold Standard, these requirements shift forest carbon from extractive offsetting purchasing cheap credits from vulnerable communities toward partnership models recognizing indigenous knowledge and governance as essential to long-term carbon storage. Her analysis shows that projects respecting tenure security achieve lower reversal rates because local stakeholders have incentives to maintain forests receiving ongoing benefits rather than viewing them as external assets vulnerable to appropriation.

Her current focus addresses the voluntary carbon market integrity collapse triggering corporate retreat from offsetting. Investigations revealed widespread over-crediting in avoided deforestation projects, renewable energy certificates from installations that would have been built anyway, and reforestation promises vulnerable to drought and fire. Rather than abandoning the instrument, Streck proposes structural reforms: independent verification replacing self-certification, transparent public registries enabling third-party auditing, conservative baselines assuming business-as-usual continuation absent carbon revenue, and buffer pools insuring against reversal risks. Her Carbon Market Integrity Pact, launched at COP30, commits 40+ major corporations to procure only credits meeting these criteria, creating demand-side pressure for quality supply while signaling to regulators that private sector support exists for mandatory standards separating legitimate complementary offsetting from greenwashing substitution. She argues that rejecting all offsets throws away the only mechanism capable of mobilizing private capital at the $4-5 trillion annual scale needed — instead, we need rigorous standards enabling high-integrity credits from nature-based solutions and engineered removals to complement, not replace, direct emission reductions in corporate net zero pathways.

At 55+, Streck remains focused on operationalizing Article 6 at scale while defending market integrity against political shortcuts. Her recent publications analyze early bilateral agreements extracting lessons on authorization timelines, registry technical specifications, and revenue distribution formulas informing subsequent deals. She warns that slow implementation risks losing the 2020s window when rapid deployment is most critical, urging parties to prioritize pragmatic functionality over perfect design while maintaining non-negotiable integrity requirements. Her open-source Article 6 Readiness Toolkit helps developing nations establish national frameworks for authorizing ITMO issuance, addressing the capacity gap preventing Global South participation in carbon finance flows historically captured by intermediaries. Her through-line across two decades: climate change is an economic externality requiring economic solutions, and the path to net zero runs through markets that put a price on carbon, mobilize private capital at scale, and direct investment toward the cheapest tons abated first — whether in Bangalore or Berlin — while maintaining accounting integrity ensuring every credited reduction represents real atmospheric benefit, respecting human rights ensuring climate action doesn't reproduce colonial extraction patterns, and building institutional trust enabling sustained political support for the decades-long transformation ahead.
