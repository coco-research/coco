# Jessica Litman

Jessica Litman is a leading copyright scholar whose work examines the historical development of copyright law and its application to digital technologies, particularly focusing on library rights and fair use doctrine. As Professor at University of Michigan Law School, she combines archival research with contemporary policy analysis to argue for copyright frameworks that maintain balance between creator incentives and public access. Her central insight challenges the assumption that broader copyright protection always benefits creators: when exclusive rights expand beyond what is necessary to incentivize creation, they impede the very dissemination and building upon existing works that copyright was designed to encourage.

Her book "Digital Copyright" traced how each new technology enabling easier copying—from the printing press to photocopiers to digital networks—triggered industry panic followed by eventual adaptation. Rather than treating digital copying as fundamentally different, Litman demonstrates that copyright law has repeatedly evolved to accommodate technological change while maintaining core balances. When publishers feared photocopiers would destroy their business models, courts recognized fair use for educational copying and libraries developed systematic approaches to reproduction. Similarly, digital lending by libraries can operate within copyright's existing framework if properly structured to mirror physical lending limitations.

On controlled digital lending, Litman provides detailed legal analysis showing how libraries can digitize physical collections and lend digital copies under terms ensuring one-to-one ownership ratios: for each physical copy owned, one digital copy may be lent at a time, preventing unlimited duplication. This approach maintains copyright holder markets while enabling libraries to serve patrons who increasingly expect digital access. Her framework influenced the Internet Archive's National Emergency Library during COVID-19 and ongoing CDL initiatives despite publisher litigation challenging these practices.

Litman's historical research on fair use demonstrates that this doctrine evolved from British common law principles allowing abridgment and commentary to modern statutory factors balancing purpose, nature, amount, and market effect. Courts have consistently recognized that transformative uses serving different purposes than originals deserve greater protection even if they affect potential licensing markets. When Google scanned books to create searchable databases, the resulting use was transformative because it enabled discovery rather than replacing reading. Similarly, library digitization for preservation and access serves different functions than commercial exploitation of copyrighted works.

At 60+, Litman remains focused on emerging challenges in AI and machine learning: when large language models train on copyrighted works, does this constitute fair use or does it require licensing? She argues that training for purposes of pattern recognition and statistical analysis is transformative because it extracts facts and relationships rather than copying expressive content for consumption. However, she acknowledges that outputs reproducing substantial portions of training data raise different questions requiring careful case-by-case analysis. Through three decades of scholarship, her message remains consistent: copyright law succeeds when it adapts to technological change while maintaining constitutional balance between incentives and access, ensuring that future creators can build upon the cultural heritage that past generations produced.
