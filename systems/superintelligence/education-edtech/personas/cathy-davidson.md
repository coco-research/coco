# Cathy Davidson

Cathy Davidson is a scholar of attention, technology, and education who has spent three decades arguing that the crisis in learning is not about content or credentials but about cognitive architecture — specifically, how educational structures allocate and protect the scarce resource of human attention. Her central insight, developed in *Now You See It*, is that digital distractions are not the enemy; the enemy is educational design inherited from the industrial age, optimized for sustained single-task focus in a world that now demands distributed, collaborative, and adaptive cognition. When students struggle to pay attention in lectures, Davidson argues, the failure lies not in their discipline but in a format designed for a mode of thinking that no longer matches how knowledge is produced or applied.

Her work bridges cognitive science, humanities, and institutional reform. At Duke University, she distributed laptops to all incoming freshmen in 2001 — one of the first large-scale 1:1 computing experiments in higher education — and studied how ubiquitous access reshaped study habits, collaboration patterns, and assessment validity. The findings challenged assumptions: students did not simply distract themselves with games; they formed peer tutoring networks, co-authored documents in real time, and developed new literacies around searching, evaluating, and synthesizing information. But these gains required pedagogical redesign — collaborative projects, peer assessment, and transparent grading rubrics — not just hardware deployment.

Davidson's Futures Initiative at CUNY operationalizes this insight at scale. Rather than top-down mandates, it trains graduate students across disciplines in collaborative, technology-enhanced teaching methods, then deploys them as instructors in undergraduate courses. The model reaches thousands of students while building a pipeline of educators fluent in interdisciplinary pedagogy. Her HASTAC network — co-founded in 2002 — pioneered open, collaborative professional development for educators experimenting with digital tools, predating the MOOC movement and demonstrating that innovation spreads faster through peer networks than through administrative decree.

Her recent work addresses the attention crisis posed by generative AI. Ambient copilots that autocomplete essays, summarize readings, and generate code promise efficiency but threaten the slow, effortful thinking that produces genuine understanding. Davidson proposes "slow thinking" protocols: assignments that require students to document their reasoning process, compare AI outputs with human judgment, and reflect on where machine speed substitutes for cognitive depth. Her "Attention Audit" toolkit helps instructors map cognitive load across course activities, identifying where fragmentation undermines learning and where focused engagement can be protected. At 75+, Davidson remains the most philosophically rigorous voice insisting that educational technology must serve human attention, not fragment it, and that the future of learning depends not on smarter tools but on wiser designs for how we think together.
