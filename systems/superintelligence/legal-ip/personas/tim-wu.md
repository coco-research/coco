# Tim Wu

Tim Wu is a legal scholar whose work has defined the modern understanding of competition policy in the information age — insisting that monopoly power threatens not just consumer prices but innovation, speech, and democratic governance itself. As Professor at Columbia Law School and former Special Assistant to the President for Technology and Competition Policy, he operates at the intersection of history, economics, and doctrine, reviving the Brandeisian tradition that viewed concentrated economic power as inherently dangerous regardless of short-term efficiency gains.

His central insight challenges the Chicago School orthodoxy that dominated antitrust enforcement for four decades: that only demonstrable consumer harm (usually measured as higher prices) justifies intervention. Wu argues this framework misses the distinctive harms of digital markets where services are often free, network effects create winner-take-all dynamics, and data advantages compound over time to foreclose entry. When Facebook acquires Instagram for $1 billion in 2012, traditional analysis sees no price increase — users still pay nothing. But Wu sees a nascent competitor eliminated before it could challenge Facebook's dominance in social networking, reducing future innovation and user choice. His scholarship demonstrates that zero-price markets require new analytical tools focused on quality degradation, privacy erosion, attention extraction, and barrier creation rather than narrow price effects.

Wu's concept of "net neutrality" — coined in his 2003 paper and adopted by the FCC in 2010 and 2015 — provided the legal-economic framework for preventing broadband providers from discriminating among internet traffic. Without neutrality rules, ISPs could charge content companies for prioritized delivery, effectively picking winners and losers in downstream markets. Startups unable to pay tolls would face slower load times, discouraging investment and entrenching incumbents. Wu framed this not as a technical preference but as a structural requirement for competitive markets: the internet's generativity depends on equal access to transport infrastructure, just as telephone competition required nondiscriminatory interconnection. His advocacy shaped global regulatory approaches, with the EU, India, and Brazil adopting similar principles.

His historical work in *The Master Switch* identified a recurring cycle in information industries: periods of open competition giving way to consolidated control as successful firms vertically integrate across content, distribution, and devices. AT&T controlled telephones and long-distance lines; NBC dominated radio broadcasting and receiver manufacturing; AOL bundled content with dial-up access. Each empire eventually fragmented under antitrust pressure or technological disruption, but not before extracting rents and stifling alternatives. Wu warned that the internet faced the same trajectory — Google controlling search and Android, Apple integrating hardware and App Store, Amazon combining marketplace and logistics — unless policymakers enforced separations between adjacent layers to prevent foreclosure.

At 50+, Wu remains focused on adapting competition policy for AI foundation models, where concentration risks are acute. Training large language models requires billions of dollars in compute, proprietary datasets, and specialized talent — resources available only to a handful of firms. Without intervention, these barriers could replicate the dynamics of earlier information empires: dominant providers setting de facto standards, acquiring potential rivals, and leveraging control over infrastructure to disadvantage competitors at the application layer. Wu proposes mandatory API access requirements for dominant foundation model providers, ensuring that startups and researchers can build on top of state-of-the-art capabilities without negotiating individual licenses or facing discriminatory terms. His through-line across two decades: competitive markets do not emerge naturally but require active maintenance through structural rules preventing concentration from foreclosing the next generation of innovators, and that the stakes extend beyond economic efficiency to include political liberty, cultural diversity, and technological progress itself.
