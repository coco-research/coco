# Richard Stallman

Richard Stallman is the founder of the free software movement whose vision and technical contributions created the foundation for modern collaborative software development. As founder of the GNU Project and the Free Software Foundation, he transformed computing from a proprietary industry where users had no control over their tools into a global commons where knowledge is shared freely. His central insight challenges the assumption that software must be owned: just as recipes should not be patented, neither should algorithms be monopolized, because restricting access to knowledge harms society regardless of any claimed incentive benefits.

His launch of the GNU Project in 1983 responded to a fundamental injustice he witnessed at MIT's AI Lab: when proprietary software replaced the collaborative culture of sharing code, users became dependent on vendors who controlled their computational experience. Stallman recognized that this dependency gave developers power over users that violated basic ethical principles. His solution was revolutionary: instead of accepting proprietary control, he would build a complete alternative where every user had the four essential freedoms — to run the program for any purpose, to study how it works, to modify it to suit their needs, and to redistribute copies either original or modified. This was not about getting software for free but about ensuring users maintained control over their own computing.

The GNU General Public License embodied Stallman's insight that legal mechanisms could protect freedom as effectively as they protected property. By using copyright law to require that derivative works maintain the same freedoms as the original, copyleft created a self-perpetuating commons that could not be enclosed by corporate interests. When companies attempted to incorporate GPL-licensed code into proprietary products without sharing modifications, the license's enforcement mechanisms ensured compliance or required relicensing. This "jiu-jitsu" approach turned intellectual property law against itself, using the very mechanisms designed to restrict access to guarantee universal access instead.

Stallman's technical contributions matched his philosophical leadership: Emacs became the most extensible text editor ever created, demonstrating that free software could exceed proprietary alternatives in functionality and customization. GCC provided a portable compiler infrastructure that enabled cross-platform development long before such capabilities were common in commercial tools. These were not mere utilities but proof-of-concept demonstrations that collaborative development without proprietary constraints could produce superior results through collective intelligence rather than individual ownership.

At 70+, Stallman remains focused on emerging threats to user freedom: cloud computing creates situations where users never possess the software running on their behalf, circumventing traditional distribution-based freedom guarantees. His advocacy for the Affero GPL addressed this by requiring source disclosure even for network services, though he acknowledges that true freedom in cloud environments requires architectural changes beyond licensing. Similarly, his opposition to software patents continues despite mainstream acceptance, arguing that patents on mathematical ideas obstruct innovation rather than promote it. Through five decades of advocacy, his message remains consistent: software freedom is not negotiable because it is fundamentally about human freedom — once we accept that others may control our computational tools, we accept a form of digital servitude incompatible with autonomy and dignity.
