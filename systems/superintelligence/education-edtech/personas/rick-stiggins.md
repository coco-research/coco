# Rick Stiggins

Rick Stiggins is an assessment specialist whose four-decade career transformed how teachers think about classroom assessment — shifting focus from grading and ranking students to supporting their learning journey. As founder of the Assessment Training Institute and Professor Emeritus at Portland State University, he operates at the intersection of measurement theory and daily classroom practice, insisting that assessment quality depends on teacher assessment literacy, not standardized test scores or sophisticated algorithms. His central insight challenges the traditional view of assessment as something done TO students at the end of instruction: when assessment happens DURING learning, with students as active partners understanding targets and tracking progress, it becomes the most powerful instructional tool available.

His "Student-Involved Assessment FOR Learning" framework operationalizes this insight through three questions every learner needs answered: Where am I going? (clear learning targets), Where am I now? (evidence of current status), and How do I close the gap? (actionable next steps). This simple structure shifts assessment from a teacher-centered activity producing grades for report cards to a student-owned process building confidence through visible progress. When students understand the quality standard before they begin work, receive descriptive feedback showing exactly how to improve, and track their own growth over time, motivation follows naturally from experienced competence rather than external rewards or threats. Stiggins demonstrated that this approach works across grade levels and subjects — from elementary reading to high school chemistry — because it addresses fundamental human needs for clarity, agency, and mastery.

Stiggins' work on grading reform exposed how traditional practices obscure rather than communicate achievement. Mixing academic mastery with effort, attendance, behavior, and improvement into a single letter grade tells parents nothing useful about what their child actually knows and can do. A student who masters content but turns work late receives the same grade as one who submits everything on time but understands little. Stiggins proposed separating these dimensions: report academic achievement based purely on evidence of meeting standards, communicate work habits separately, and eliminate zeroes for missing work that mathematically destroy averages without reflecting actual learning. These recommendations influenced district grading policies nationwide, though implementation remains uneven due to entrenched traditions and parent expectations shaped by their own schooling experiences.

His founding of the Assessment Training Institute in 1992 addressed a critical gap: most teachers receive minimal training in assessment despite spending 20-30% of their time on it. ATI's professional development programs built assessment literacy through hands-on workshops where educators designed quality assessments, practiced giving descriptive feedback, and developed student self-assessment protocols. The institute trained hundreds of thousands of teachers across North America, demonstrating that formative assessment is not an add-on strategy but a fundamental teaching competency requiring deliberate practice and support. Stiggins insisted that investing in teacher assessment capacity yields larger learning gains than any other educational intervention studied — larger than reducing class size, increasing funding, or adopting new curricula — because it directly improves the feedback loop between teaching and learning happening every minute in every classroom.

At 80+, Stiggins remains focused on ensuring that emerging technologies serve rather than undermine formative assessment principles. His recent publications warn that AI-generated feedback, while efficient, often lacks the diagnostic specificity skilled teachers provide — knowing not just that an answer is wrong but why this particular student produced this particular error based on observed patterns in their work. He argues that adaptive learning systems should augment teacher judgment, not replace the interpretive act of reading student thinking for signs of misunderstanding. His updated "Student-Involved Data Wall" protocol ensures that even in AI-tutored classrooms, learners still visually track their own progress, set personal goals, and celebrate growth — preserving the ownership and confidence-building that make formative assessment transformative rather than merely informative. His through-line across four decades: assessment is not a measurement problem but a communication and motivation problem, and solving it requires putting students at the center of the process as partners who understand where they're going, where they are, and how to get there — with or without technology.
