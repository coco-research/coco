# Michael Crow

Michael Crow is the president of Arizona State University who has spent over two decades proving that a public research university can simultaneously expand access, raise academic standards, and drive economic innovation — if it is willing to redesign itself from first principles. When he arrived at ASU in 2002, the university enrolled 45,000 students and was known primarily as a party school. Today, ASU enrolls over 130,000 students across physical and digital campuses, ranks among the top US universities for innovation, and graduates more students from low-income backgrounds than all Ivy League schools combined.

Crow's transformation rests on what he calls the "New American University" model, codified in eight design aspirations that reject the traditional tradeoff between exclusivity and excellence. His central insight is that exclusion is not a marker of quality but a failure of institutional design. If a university cannot deliver excellent outcomes to a broad population, the problem lies in its structures, pedagogy, and resource allocation — not in the students it admits. This philosophy drove ASU to invest heavily in adaptive learning technologies, predictive analytics for student success, and partnerships with employers to align curricula with workforce needs.

His eAdvisor system, which uses machine learning to flag students at risk of falling behind and recommends interventions, improved four-year graduation rates by 30% while reducing time-to-degree. Rather than replacing human advisors, the technology augmented their capacity to focus on high-need cases. Similarly, ASU's partnerships with companies like Starbucks, Uber, and the US military created pathways for non-traditional learners to earn degrees while working — demonstrating that flexibility and rigor can coexist when institutions stop treating campus residency as the only valid mode of learning.

Crow is characteristically blunt about academic tradition, calling it "peer pressure from dead people" and insisting that every practice — from tenure criteria to lecture formats — justify itself against current evidence of what works. His recent work addresses the disruption posed by generative AI, arguing that universities must shift from policing plagiarism to verifying competency through performance-based assessments that AI cannot easily fake. At 70+, Crow remains the most influential voice in higher education reform, urging institutions to measure themselves by whom they include and how well those students succeed, not by how many applicants they reject.
