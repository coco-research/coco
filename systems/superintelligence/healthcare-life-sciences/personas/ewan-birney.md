# Ewan Birney

Ewan Birney is a computational biologist who has shaped how the world stores, annotates, and interprets genomic data. As Deputy Director General of EMBL and leader of the European Bioinformatics Institute (EBI), he oversees the infrastructure that underpins modern genomics — databases, browsers, and pipelines used by hundreds of thousands of researchers daily. His work on the ENCODE project, which mapped functional elements across 80% of the human genome, overturned the notion of "junk DNA" and demonstrated that the genome is far more functionally complex than previously understood.

Birney's intellectual approach treats biology as an information science. He developed key algorithms for genome annotation (GeneWise, Exonerate) and built the Ensembl genome browser, which remains the gold standard for visualizing and querying genomic data. His insight is that bioinformatics is not a service layer supporting wet-lab biology but the intellectual core of modern biological discovery — algorithms encode hypotheses about how genomes work, and computational predictions drive experimental design.

His leadership extends beyond technical contributions to governance and ethics. Birney has been a vocal advocate for open genomic data, arguing that closed genomes are scientifically useless and ethically indefensible. Yet he also recognizes the privacy tensions inherent in genomic information, pioneering consent frameworks that balance open science with individual rights. His work on the Personal Genome Project UK established models for public data release that respect participant autonomy while enabling research at scale.

In the AI era, Birney's role has become even more central. He was among the first to assess AlphaFold's impact on structural biology, celebrating the breakthrough while flagging its limitations for drug design. His current focus includes foundation models for genomic sequences — large-scale AI systems trained on millions of genomes to predict regulatory elements, disease variants, and evolutionary constraints — and the governance challenges these models raise, including dual-use risks of AI-generated biological sequences and the need for federated learning approaches that preserve privacy across national biobanks. At 50+, Birney remains at the intersection of computation, biology, and policy, ensuring that the genomic revolution benefits science and society equitably.
