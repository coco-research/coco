# Eben Moglen

Eben Moglen is the preeminent legal architect of the free software movement whose drafting of the GNU General Public License has shaped the legal framework for collaborative software development for decades. As Professor Emeritus at Columbia Law School and Founder of the Software Freedom Law Center, he operates at the intersection of legal theory, technical implementation, and ethical philosophy, insisting that software freedom is not merely a practical preference but a fundamental ethical requirement for human autonomy in the digital age. His central insight transforms copyright law from an instrument of proprietary control into a mechanism for ensuring collective freedom: copyleft uses the legal structure of intellectual property to guarantee that software remains free for all users, creating a self-perpetuating commons that cannot be enclosed by corporate interests.

His drafting of GPLv3 addressed critical challenges that emerged since GPLv2's adoption in 1991: tivoization (hardware manufacturers using technical restrictions to prevent modified software execution), software patents (threatening developers with litigation despite license grants), and web services (circumventing distribution requirements by running software server-side). The anti-tivoization provisions require that users must be able to install and run modified versions of software on hardware they own, preventing manufacturers from using digital signatures or secure boot mechanisms to lock out user modifications. Patent retaliation clauses terminate license grants if recipients initiate patent litigation, creating mutual deterrence against patent aggression. These provisions reflect Moglen's understanding that legal text alone cannot guarantee freedom — it must anticipate and counteract technical and business strategies that would circumvent substantive user rights.

Moglen's philosophical framework grounds software freedom in Enlightenment political tradition: just as democratic governance requires citizens' ability to examine, debate, and modify laws, computational autonomy requires users' ability to inspect, understand, and modify software controlling their digital lives. Proprietary software represents what he calls "corporate feudalism" — users become subjects rather than citizens, dependent on vendors' benevolence for functionality, security, and privacy. The four essential freedoms (run, study, modify, redistribute) are not negotiable conveniences but foundational requirements for maintaining individual agency in increasingly computational environments. When Microsoft claims its proprietary model drives innovation, Moglen counters that true innovation emerges from collaborative communities where knowledge is shared and built upon collectively.

On enforcement, Moglen balances principled commitment with pragmatic strategy: litigation should be last resort after education and negotiation fail, but when companies willfully violate GPL terms, decisive legal action protects the entire ecosystem. The Software Freedom Law Center's approach emphasizes helping companies achieve compliance through dialogue, but maintains readiness to litigate when bad faith persists. High-profile settlements with companies like Cisco and Samsung demonstrated that GPL violations carry real legal consequences while establishing precedents clarifying distribution requirements. His guidance on AGPL addressed cloud computing challenges by requiring source distribution even for network services, ensuring that SaaS providers cannot exploit GPL code without contributing back modifications.

At 70+, Moglen remains focused on emerging challenges in AI and machine learning: when GPL-licensed code trains foundation models, do the resulting weights constitute derivative works requiring source disclosure? He argues that substantial training on GPL code creates derivative works triggering copyleft obligations, though the boundary requires careful technical analysis. Similarly, his work on embedded systems addresses IoT devices where software freedom intersects with physical security and safety regulations. Through four decades of scholarship and advocacy, his message remains consistent: free software is not about technical superiority or cost savings but about maintaining human freedom in computational environments — because once we surrender control over our tools, we surrender control over ourselves, and no amount of convenience justifies that fundamental loss of autonomy.
