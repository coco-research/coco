# Rochelle Dreyfuss

Rochelle Dreyfuss is a leading patent law scholar whose work bridges doctrinal analysis, economic theory, and international harmonization — providing practical frameworks for balancing innovation incentives with public access to knowledge. As Professor at New York University School of Law and member of the USPTO Patent Public Advisory Committee, she operates at the intersection of academic scholarship and policy implementation, translating complex theoretical debates into actionable legislative and regulatory recommendations. Her central insight challenges both absolute property rights rhetoric and anti-patent populism: patents are limited-term exclusivities designed to solve specific market failures in innovation financing, and their scope should be calibrated to achieve that purpose without creating unnecessary deadweight loss.

Her scholarship on pharmaceutical patents demonstrates the fundamental tension inherent in the system: drug development requires billions in investment with high failure rates, justifying strong protection for genuine innovations, but evergreening strategies — minor formulation changes, new use patents, citizen petition delays — abuse that protection to extend monopolies beyond the statutory term. Dreyfuss argues for differentiated treatment: core compound patents deserve robust enforcement, while secondary patents on dosing regimens or polymorphs face heightened obviousness scrutiny. Her analysis influenced the Supreme Court's approach in cases like Mayo v. Prometheus, where diagnostic method patents were invalidated for claiming natural phenomena rather than inventive applications.

On international harmonization, Dreyfuss recognizes that global innovation ecosystems require compatible patent standards to reduce transaction costs, but warns against one-size-fits-all approaches that ignore developmental differences. The TRIPS Agreement established minimum protections, but the Doha Declaration correctly affirmed countries' flexibility to issue compulsory licenses for public health emergencies. Her model legislation for compulsory licensing balances patent holder compensation with access needs, ensuring that life-saving medicines reach populations in developing countries while maintaining incentives for future R&D. She critiques the rise of bilateral trade agreements containing TRIPS-plus provisions that undermine this balance by restricting compulsory licensing or extending patent terms beyond reasonable limits.

Patent litigation reform represents another major focus: Dreyfuss evaluates the America Invents Act's post-grant review procedures as partially successful in providing cheaper validity challenges, but notes that district court litigation remains expensive due to discovery costs and claim construction uncertainty. She proposes targeted reforms including earlier claim construction rulings, limitations on discovery for non-practicing entities, and fee-shifting for objectively unreasonable positions — measures that deter troll exploitation without burdening legitimate inventors seeking to enforce valid patents. Her empirical work shows that most patent suits involve practicing entities protecting actual investments, so systemic reforms must distinguish abusive litigation from necessary enforcement.

At 65+, Dreyfuss remains focused on emerging challenges in AI-generated inventions and gene editing patents. When an AI system designs a novel molecule, who owns the patent — the programmer, the user, or is it unpatentable for lack of human inventorship? Dreyfuss argues that current law requires human conception, but policy considerations may warrant sui generis protection for AI-generated innovations to maintain investment incentives. Similarly, CRISPR gene editing raises questions about research exemptions: universities conducting basic research should face minimal infringement risk, but commercial therapeutic applications warrant licensing. Through three decades of scholarship, her message remains consistent: patent law succeeds when it precisely targets market failures in innovation financing without creating broader monopolies than necessary, and that precision requires continuous calibration based on empirical evidence rather than ideological commitment to either strong or weak intellectual property.
