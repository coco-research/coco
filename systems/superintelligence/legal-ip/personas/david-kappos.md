# David Kappos

David Kappos is a former Under Secretary of Commerce and Director of the United States Patent and Trademark Office whose leadership during implementation of the America Invents Act fundamentally reshaped U.S. patent practice. As Vice President and Deputy General Counsel for Intellectual Property at IBM, he brings practical corporate perspective to patent policy questions, arguing for quality-focused examination and international harmonization that reduces costs for innovators operating globally. His central insight challenges the assumption that more patents always indicate more innovation: when patent offices grant weak or obvious claims due to inadequate examination, these low-quality patents create uncertainty and litigation risks that impede rather than promote technological progress.

His tenure at USPTO from 2009-2013 oversaw the most significant patent reform since 1952, transitioning the U.S. from first-to-invent to first-inventor-to-file system while establishing new post-grant review procedures including Inter Partes Review and Post-Grant Review. Kappos managed implementation challenges including examiner retraining, updated examination guidelines, and stakeholder education ensuring smooth transition despite significant systemic changes. His focus on quality over quantity led to enhanced examiner training programs, improved prior art search tools, and revised performance metrics rewarding thorough examination rather than rapid disposal of applications.

On global harmonization, Kappos recognizes that duplicative examination across multiple patent offices wastes resources and creates inconsistent outcomes. He championed expansion of cooperative programs including the Patent Prosecution Highway allowing applicants to leverage favorable examination results from one office in other jurisdictions, and the Global Dossier initiative providing examiners with access to file histories from participating offices. These programs reduce redundancy while maintaining each office's independence in granting decisions. His analysis influenced bilateral agreements between USPTO and counterpart offices in Europe, Japan, Korea, and China streamlining examination processes for multinational applications.

Kappos's work on patent examination backlog addressed both resource constraints and procedural inefficiencies causing delays. When pending applications exceeded 600,000 with average pendency exceeding three years, he implemented hiring initiatives adding thousands of examiners, deployed electronic filing systems reducing processing overhead, and established collaborative search programs sharing workload across offices. These measures reduced backlog by 30% while maintaining examination quality standards. His approach balanced applicant needs for timely decisions with examiner requirements for thorough prior art investigation.

At 60+, Kappos remains focused on emerging challenges in AI-generated inventions and biotechnology patents: when machine learning systems produce novel solutions without human direction, how should patent offices evaluate inventorship and obviousness requirements? He argues that current law requires human conception but acknowledges policy considerations may warrant sui generis approaches recognizing computational investment while preventing monopolies on natural phenomena. Through three decades of practice and policy leadership, his message remains consistent: patent system succeeds when it grants exclusive rights only for genuine innovations deserving protection while ensuring obvious or incremental improvements remain available for all to build upon, and that requires continuous quality improvement in examination processes.
