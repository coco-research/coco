# Henry Derwent

Henry Derwent is a climate policy architect whose three-decade career built the financial infrastructure enabling global decarbonization through carbon pricing and emissions trading. As former President of the International Emissions Trading Association (IETA) and founder of Climate Strategy Partners, he operates at the intersection of treaty negotiation, market design, and corporate strategy, insisting that the gap between climate ambition and climate action is fundamentally a capital allocation problem solvable through well-designed market mechanisms. His central insight challenges both free-market skeptics who reject all regulation and environmental purists who reject all offsets: carbon markets, when structured with robust integrity standards, are the most efficient tool for directing trillions in private capital toward lowest-cost abatement opportunities worldwide.

His leadership during the EU Emissions Trading System's Phase III redesign transformed Europe's flagship climate policy from a failed experiment into an effective price signal. When he joined EU negotiations in the late 2000s, the EU ETS was drowning in surplus allowances priced below €5 per ton — too cheap to drive investment in clean technology. Derwent architected the Market Stability Reserve, a mechanism automatically withdrawing excess permits from circulation when supply exceeded demand, restoring scarcity and pushing prices above €80 per ton by 2023. This price signal triggered €40+ billion in annual abatement investment across power generation, heavy industry, and aviation, demonstrating that cap-and-trade works when designed with automatic stabilizers rather than political discretion. The EU ETS now covers 40% of European emissions and generates €20+ billion annually auctioned to fund member state climate programs — proof that carbon pricing can simultaneously reduce emissions, raise revenue, and maintain industrial competitiveness through border adjustment mechanisms.

Derwent's most consequential contribution came during Paris Agreement negotiations, where he led technical working groups drafting Article 6 — the provisions governing international carbon markets and cooperative approaches. The Glasgow Rulebook finalized in 2021 established two pathways: Article 6.2 enabling bilateral trades of Internationally Transferred Mitigation Outcomes (ITMOs) between countries, and Article 6.4 creating a centralized UN crediting mechanism replacing the Kyoto Protocol's Clean Development Mechanism. Critically, Derwent insisted on "corresponding adjustments" — requiring host countries to deduct exported credits from their own NDC accounting to prevent double-counting where both buyer and seller claim the same emission reduction. This accounting rigor, opposed by some developing nations fearing it would reduce their flexibility, ensures environmental integrity while enabling private sector participation at scale. Early implementations show 15 countries operationalizing 6.2 transfers, with projections of $250+ billion in annual private climate finance flows by 2030 once liquidity develops.

His current focus addresses the corporate net zero credibility crisis. Hundreds of companies have pledged carbon neutrality by 2050, but investigations reveal widespread reliance on low-quality offsets — avoided deforestation credits lacking additionality, renewable energy certificates from projects that would have happened anyway, reforestation promises vulnerable to wildfires and reversal. Derwent's High Integrity Carbon Credits Framework establishes clear criteria: additionality (emission reductions wouldn't occur without carbon revenue), permanence (carbon stored for 100+ years or insured against reversal), leakage prevention (project doesn't shift emissions elsewhere), and social safeguards (indigenous rights respected, local communities benefit). He argues that rejecting all offsets throws away the only mechanism capable of mobilizing private capital at the $4-5 trillion annual scale needed — instead, we need rigorous standards separating legitimate complementary offsetting from greenwashing substitution. His Carbon Market Integrity Alliance, launched at COP30, commits 50+ major corporations to procure only credits meeting these criteria, creating demand-side pressure for quality supply.

At 60+, Derwent remains focused on operationalizing Article 6 at scale. His recent publications analyze early bilateral agreements — Switzerland funding Peru's electric bus fleet, Sweden supporting Ghana's cookstove distribution — extracting lessons on authorization processes, registry interoperability, and revenue sharing that inform subsequent deals. He warns that slow implementation risks losing the 2020s window when rapid deployment is most critical, urging parties to prioritize pragmatic functionality over perfect design. His open-source Article 6 Readiness Toolkit helps developing nations establish national frameworks for authorizing ITMO issuance, addressing the capacity gap preventing Global South participation. His through-line across three decades: climate change is an economic externality requiring economic solutions, and the path to net zero runs through markets that put a price on carbon, mobilize private capital at scale, and direct investment toward the cheapest tons abated first — whether in Bangalore or Berlin — while maintaining accounting integrity that ensures every credited reduction represents real atmospheric benefit.
