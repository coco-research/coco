# Daniel Solove

Daniel Solove is a legal scholar whose work has defined the modern understanding of privacy — not as a vague right to be left alone but as a constellation of distinct interests threatened by specific technological practices. As John Marshall Harlan Research Professor at George Washington University Law School and founder of TeachPrivacy, he operates at the intersection of philosophy, technology, and doctrine, insisting that effective privacy protection requires precise diagnosis of harms rather than rhetorical appeals to secrecy or anonymity.

His landmark article "A Taxonomy of Privacy" identified 16 distinct privacy harms across four dimensions: information collection (surveillance, interrogation), information processing (aggregation, identification, insecurity, secondary use, exclusion), information dissemination (breach of confidentiality, disclosure, exposure, increased accessibility, blackmail, appropriation, distortion), and invasion (intrusion, decisional interference). This framework replaced vague notions of "privacy" with actionable categories that courts, legislators, and technologists could address specifically. When a company aggregates browsing data to infer sensitive traits, that's an aggregation harm. When a database is hacked due to negligent security, that's an insecurity harm. Each requires different remedies — transparency for aggregation, liability for insecurity, consent for secondary use. His taxonomy became the standard reference for privacy law worldwide, cited in thousands of judicial opinions and academic articles.

Solove's book *Nothing to Hide* dismantled the pervasive argument that privacy concerns are irrelevant if you have "nothing to hide." He demonstrated that this framing misunderstands privacy's function: it is not about concealing wrongdoing but about maintaining autonomy, preventing power asymmetries, and preserving space for dissent, experimentation, and intimacy. Surveillance chills behavior even when no one is actively watching — people self-censor, avoid controversial associations, and conform to perceived norms when they know their actions might be scrutinized later. This chilling effect undermines democracy regardless of whether any individual commits crimes. His analysis shifted the debate from moral suspicion ("why do you need privacy unless you're guilty?") to structural concern ("how does ubiquitous observation alter power dynamics?").

His work on reputation examined how digital permanence and virality create new forms of social control. A single embarrassing photo, taken out of context, can circulate globally within hours and remain searchable forever — destroying careers, relationships, and mental health. Traditional defamation law struggles to address this because the statements may be technically true, and the speakers may lack malicious intent. Solove proposed legal reforms balancing free speech with protection from disproportionate shaming: stronger rights to de-index outdated information, narrower immunity for platforms that amplify harassment, and recognition of "digital dignity" as a protected interest. These ideas influenced the EU's Right to Be Forgotten ruling and California's eraser law for minors.

At 50+, Solove remains focused on adapting privacy frameworks for the AI era. Foundation models trained on trillions of tokens scraped from the web raise novel questions: Does using publicly available data violate contextual integrity? Can individuals opt out of having their words embedded in model weights? How do we audit opaque systems for privacy violations when the training process itself is proprietary? He proposes "contextual integrity" audits requiring developers to document data sources, evaluate whether uses align with original contexts, and establish redress mechanisms for unexpected exposures. His TeachPrivacy platform translates these principles into practical training for millions of employees, teaching them to recognize phishing, manage passwords, and comply with regulations — bridging the gap between high-level theory and daily operational reality. His through-line across three decades: privacy is not a luxury for the secretive but a prerequisite for autonomy, and protecting it requires precise tools matched to specific harms rather than blanket slogans or technological silver bullets.
