# Mateo Jaramillo

Mateo Jaramillo is an engineer and entrepreneur whose work addresses the critical missing piece in the clean energy transition: multi-day energy storage. As co-founder and CEO of Form Energy and former VP of Engineering for Energy Products at Tesla, he operates at the intersection of electrochemistry, grid economics, and manufacturing scale-up, insisting that lithium-ion batteries — while revolutionary for electric vehicles and short-duration grid services — cannot economically solve the multi-day storage problem required for 100% renewable grids. His central insight challenges the assumption that one battery chemistry fits all applications: the grid needs different tools for different timescales. Lithium-ion excels at 4-hour shifting (solar noon to evening peak) but becomes prohibitively expensive for 100-hour duration (surviving week-long weather droughts when wind and solar output drops). For that regime, cost per stored kilowatt-hour matters more than round-trip efficiency or energy density — and iron-air chemistry offers a path to $20/kWh levelized cost, undercutting natural gas peaker plants for reliability services.

His technical innovation centers on reversing the rusting process. Iron oxidizes naturally, releasing energy — this is corrosion, usually a problem. Jaramillo's research demonstrated that in alkaline electrolytes with optimized electrode architectures, this reaction becomes reversible: applying electricity converts rust back to metallic iron, storing energy; discharging converts iron back to rust, releasing energy. The chemistry uses only iron, water, and air — the three most abundant materials on Earth — eliminating supply chain constraints plaguing lithium, nickel, and cobalt batteries. While round-trip efficiency (~50%) lags lithium-ion (~90%), the ultra-low material cost ($5/kg for iron vs. $50+/kg for lithium carbonate) makes the economics work for long-duration applications where efficiency losses are acceptable tradeoffs for duration affordability. A 100-hour iron-air battery costing $20/kWh delivers cheaper reliability than a 4-hour lithium battery costing $150/kWh cycled 25 times, because the latter requires massive overbuilding to cover multi-day gaps.

Jaramillo's experience at Tesla shaped this systems-level perspective. Leading deployment of Powerpack and Megapack systems globally, he proved lithium-ion's viability for frequency regulation, peak shaving, and renewable firming at 4-hour scales. But modeling high-renewable grid scenarios revealed the "dunkelflaute" problem — German for "dark doldrums" — periods of low wind and solar lasting days or weeks during winter storms or summer heat domes. Lithium-ion cannot bridge these gaps without astronomical cost: storing 100 hours of grid demand requires 25x more lithium capacity than 4-hour shifting, multiplying costs and straining mineral supply chains already facing geopolitical concentration risks. This gap demanded a different chemistry optimized for duration rather than power, leading him to leave Tesla in 2017 and co-found Form Energy specifically targeting the 100-hour storage niche.

Form Energy's commercial validation moves from lab curiosity to grid infrastructure. The company's first pilot with Georgia Power — a 1MW/1MWh system — demonstrated stable 100-hour discharge cycles in real-world grid conditions, validating the electrochemistry outside controlled laboratory environments. Subsequent announcements include a 10MW/1GWh commercial deployment scheduled for 2026, representing the largest long-duration storage project ever contracted. Manufacturing scale-up follows Tesla's playbook: vertical integration of electrode production, automated assembly lines minimizing labor costs, and domestic sourcing of iron feedstock from US steel industry byproducts (mill scale, rust) creating circular economy synergies. The West Virginia plant under construction will produce 50GWh annually by 2028 — enough to support 5GW of 100-hour storage capacity — using abundant domestic iron rather than imported critical minerals.

At 45+, Jaramillo remains focused on scaling iron-air technology to terawatt-hour levels required for global grid decarbonization. His recent publications analyze how LDES integrates with transmission expansion, demand response, and renewable overbuilding to minimize total system costs — showing that 100-hour storage reduces curtailment by 80% and eliminates need for fossil backup capacity in high-renewable scenarios. He argues that the clean energy transition requires a portfolio of storage technologies: lithium-ion for seconds-to-hours, iron-air for days-to-weeks, hydrogen or thermal for seasonal shifting — each optimized for its duration regime rather than forcing one chemistry to serve all functions. His through-line across two decades: grid reliability in a renewable future depends not on better batteries but on right-sized batteries — matching chemistry to duration, abundance to scale, and cost to application — ensuring that the lights stay on when the sun doesn't shine and the wind doesn't blow, without burning gas or breaking the bank.
