# Mark McKenna

Mark McKenna is a leading intellectual property scholar whose work examines how trademark and patent doctrines have expanded beyond their original purposes, creating tensions with competition policy and free speech values. As Professor at University of Notre Dame Law School, he combines historical analysis with contemporary case study examination to argue for doctrinal recalibration that returns these legal frameworks to their foundational justifications. His central insight challenges the assumption that stronger IP protection always promotes innovation and consumer welfare: when trademark law protects brand investment rather than preventing confusion, or when patent-trademark overlap enables perpetual exclusivity on functional features, these expansions harm competition and reduce consumer choice.

His research on trademark law's theory of harm demonstrates how modern doctrine has drifted from its core purpose of preventing source confusion toward protecting brand owners' investments regardless of whether consumers are actually confused about product origin. The federal dilution statute, state tarnishment claims, and expansive trade dress protection all operate without requiring proof of confusion, transforming trademarks from source identifiers into valuable property rights deserving protection independent of their consumer protection function. McKenna argues for returning to confusion-based liability where plaintiffs must demonstrate actual or likely confusion about sponsorship, affiliation, or origin rather than mere similarity causing brand value transfer.

On the trademark-patent overlap problem, McKenna identifies how companies strategically use trademark law to extend exclusivity beyond patent expiration: when a product feature's patent expires, competitors should be free to copy that functional feature, but if the feature has acquired secondary meaning as a brand identifier, trademark law may prevent copying indefinitely. This creates what McKenna calls "intellectual property creep" where the shorter-term patent monopoly transforms into potentially perpetual trademark protection through the backdoor of trade dress law. His analysis influenced courts to apply more rigorous functionality tests rejecting trademark protection for features that serve utilitarian purposes regardless of brand significance.

McKenna's work on false advertising litigation examines how Lanham Act Section 43(a) provides competitors with powerful weapons to challenge each other's marketing claims, but warns that overly permissive standing rules enable anti-competitive lawsuits designed to burden rivals with litigation costs rather than correct genuine deception. He proposes requiring plaintiffs to demonstrate concrete competitive injury traceable to specific false statements rather than allowing generalized challenges to marketing puffery. Similarly, his analysis of right of publicity claims shows how celebrities increasingly seek to control expressive uses of their identity in films, music, and parody, creating tensions with First Amendment values that courts have struggled to resolve consistently.

At 50+, McKenna remains focused on emerging challenges in AI and digital branding: when AI systems generate content mimicking celebrity voices or brand styles, do existing trademark and publicity rights provide remedies, or do these situations require new sui generis protections? He argues that current doctrines stretched too far create uncertainty chilling legitimate expression, suggesting that legislative solutions may be preferable to judicial expansion of existing IP categories. Through two decades of scholarship, his message remains consistent: intellectual property law succeeds when it serves its stated purposes of promoting innovation and preventing consumer deception without creating broader monopolies than necessary, and that requires constant vigilance against doctrinal drift driven by rent-seeking rather than public interest.
