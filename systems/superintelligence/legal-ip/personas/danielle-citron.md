# Danielle Citron

Danielle Citron is a leading scholar in cyber civil rights whose work has transformed understanding of online harassment from individual rudeness to systematic discrimination undermining democratic participation. As Professor at University of Virginia School of Law, she combines legal analysis with empirical research and victim advocacy to argue that digital abuse requires structural solutions rather than individual coping strategies. Her central insight challenges the assumption that free speech values protect all online expression: when harassment campaigns silence marginalized voices through coordinated attacks, doxxing, and nonconsensual intimate imagery, they restrict rather than expand expressive freedom by driving targets out of digital public squares.

Her book "Hate Crimes in Cyberspace" documented how online harassment disproportionately affects women, people of color, LGBTQ individuals, and religious minorities, creating hostile environments that replicate offline discrimination in digital spaces. When gamergate targets received death threats, rape threats, and sustained harassment campaigns, traditional legal frameworks proved inadequate because Section 230 immunity shielded platforms from liability and criminal laws focused on physical threats rarely addressed psychological and reputational harms. Citron argues for civil rights approaches treating targeted abuse as discrimination, enabling victims to seek remedies through both civil litigation and administrative enforcement similar to employment discrimination claims.

On nonconsensual intimate imagery, Citron demonstrates how existing privacy torts fail victims because they require proving specific damages rather than recognizing inherent dignity violations. Revenge porn perpetrators exploit intimacy for humiliation, causing severe psychological harm including depression, anxiety, and suicidal ideation among victims. Citron's model legislation provides criminal penalties for distributors while creating private right of action enabling victims to obtain injunctions, damages, and attorney fees. Her framework influenced over 40 states enacting revenge porn laws and federal proposals addressing this growing problem, though she acknowledges that removal remains difficult once images spread across multiple platforms.

Citron's critique of Section 230 immunity focuses on its interpretation shielding platforms even when they algorithmically amplify harmful content or refuse to remove known nonconsensual intimate imagery. The statute was designed to protect platforms from liability for user-generated content generally, not to immunize them from responsibility when their design choices enable systematic abuse. She proposes targeted carve-outs removing immunity for platforms that knowingly facilitate targeted harassment or fail to implement reasonable safety measures like image hashing technology preventing re-upload of removed intimate imagery. This balanced approach maintains protections for legitimate user content while creating incentives for platforms to address documented harms.

At 60+, Citron remains focused on emerging challenges in AI-generated abuse: when deepfake technology creates realistic but fake intimate imagery, current laws often fail because they require proof that images depict actual victims rather than synthetic composites. Citron advocates for expanded legislation covering synthetic intimate imagery regardless of whether real individuals appear, focusing on consent violations and dignitary harms rather than traditional privacy concepts. Similarly, her work on AI-powered harassment tools examines how automated systems generate personalized abuse at scale, overwhelming traditional moderation approaches. Through two decades of scholarship, her message remains consistent: digital civil rights require proactive platform design preventing abuse rather than reactive takedown processes addressing harms after they occur, because once harassment drives marginalized voices from online spaces, the damage to democratic discourse may be irreversible.
