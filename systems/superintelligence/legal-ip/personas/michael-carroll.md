# Michael Carroll

Michael Carroll is a leading patent law scholar whose work examines how patent quality affects innovation incentives and technology dissemination across industries. As Professor at Cardozo School of Law, he combines empirical analysis with policy expertise to argue for patent system reforms that prioritize examination quality over processing speed, ensuring that granted patents represent genuine innovations worthy of exclusive rights. His central insight challenges the assumption that more patents always indicate more innovation: when patent offices grant obvious or overbroad claims due to inadequate prior art searches, these low-quality patents create uncertainty and transaction costs that impede rather than promote technological progress.

His research on patent examination quality demonstrates that USPTO resource constraints lead to insufficient prior art searches and inadequate claim scrutiny, resulting in patents that should never have issued. When examiners face production quotas requiring disposition of dozens of applications per week, they cannot thoroughly investigate whether claimed inventions represent non-obvious advances over existing knowledge. Carroll proposes increased funding for examiner training and enhanced search tools, combined with revised performance metrics rewarding quality over quantity. His analysis influenced USPTO initiatives to improve examination quality through collaborative search programs with international offices and enhanced electronic prior art databases.

On university technology transfer, Carroll examines how the Bayh-Dole Act's intention to promote commercialization of federally funded research has been distorted by institutional pressures to maximize licensing revenue. University technology transfer offices often pursue aggressive licensing strategies for blockbuster inventions while neglecting broader dissemination of less commercially promising but socially valuable technologies. Carroll argues for reorienting success metrics toward public benefit indicators including product development, job creation, and accessibility rather than purely financial returns. His framework influenced NIH guidelines for grant recipients and recommendations from academic technology transfer associations.

Carroll's work on pharmaceutical patent evergreening documents how brand-name manufacturers systematically obtain secondary patents on formulations, dosing regimens, and manufacturing processes to extend monopolies beyond expiration of core compound patents. These secondary patents often lack genuine inventive step but create substantial barriers to generic entry because potential competitors must litigate each patent individually. Carroll proposes heightened obviousness scrutiny for secondary pharmaceutical patents combined with streamlined regulatory pathways for generics challenging weak secondary claims. His analysis influenced FTC reports on pharmaceutical competition and legislative proposals addressing patent thicket problems in drug development.

At 55+, Carroll remains focused on emerging challenges in AI-generated inventions and biotechnology patents: when machine learning systems produce novel solutions without human direction, how should patent offices evaluate inventorship and obviousness? He argues that current law requires human conception but acknowledges that policy considerations may warrant sui generis approaches for AI-assisted innovations where human contribution is minimal but computational investment is substantial. Through two decades of scholarship, his message remains consistent: patent system succeeds when it grants exclusive rights only for genuine innovations that would not have been developed without incentive, while ensuring that obvious or incremental improvements remain available for all to build upon.
