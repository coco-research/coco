# Jack Balkin

Jack Balkin is a leading constitutional scholar whose work examines how First Amendment principles apply in digital environments where private platforms control information flows traditionally associated with public forums. As Professor at Yale Law School and Director of the Information Society Project, he combines constitutional theory with media studies to argue for regulatory frameworks that protect expressive freedom while addressing power imbalances created by platform concentration. His central insight challenges the assumption that Section 230 immunity and private property rights adequately protect speech: when a handful of companies control essential communication infrastructure, their design choices and moderation policies affect democratic discourse in ways requiring procedural safeguards similar to those governing state actors.

His concept of "information fiduciaries" reconceptualizes platform-user relationships by imposing duties of confidentiality, loyalty, and care on companies collecting and processing personal data. Just as doctors and lawyers owe fiduciary duties to clients whose sensitive information they handle, platforms should owe similar obligations to users whose behavioral data reveals intimate details about health, beliefs, and relationships. This framework avoids treating platforms as common carriers subject to substantive speech mandates while recognizing that data exploitation undermines user autonomy. Balkin's proposal influenced GDPR's data protection principles and California's privacy legislation, providing a middle path between pure market freedom and heavy-handed content regulation.

On network neutrality, Balkin connects broadband regulation to First Amendment values by showing how ISP control over transmission affects both speaker access and listener choice. When Comcast throttled BitTorrent traffic or Verizon blocked tethering applications, these actions implicated free speech interests even though ISPs are private companies. Balkin argues that network neutrality rules preventing discrimination among internet traffic serve First Amendment goals by ensuring diverse voices can reach audiences without gatekeeper permission. This analysis extends to platform regulation where algorithmic amplification and demonetization decisions effectively determine which speakers achieve visibility.

Balkin's work on constitutional democracy examines how digital platforms exacerbate institutional decay by enabling disinformation campaigns, foreign interference, and political polarization. Social media algorithms optimized for engagement amplify outrage and conspiracy theories because these generate more clicks, creating feedback loops that undermine shared factual premises necessary for democratic deliberation. Rather than advocating for government truth-telling powers that could enable censorship, Balkin proposes procedural reforms including algorithmic transparency, independent audit mechanisms, and user control over recommendation systems. These measures would reduce platform power to shape public discourse without requiring government determination of acceptable speech.

At 65+, Balkin remains focused on emerging challenges in AI-generated content and synthetic media: when deepfakes and large language models produce convincing but false information, how should platforms balance accuracy concerns with expressive freedom? He argues for disclosure requirements mandating clear labeling of AI-generated content combined with robust counter-speech initiatives promoting media literacy and critical thinking skills. Through three decades of scholarship, his message remains consistent: free speech in the digital age requires protecting both infrastructure access and data privacy, because without these structural safeguards, formal speech rights become meaningless when platforms control who gets heard and advertisers exploit personal information for manipulation.
