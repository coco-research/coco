# Brandon Busteed

Brandon Busteed is a researcher and innovator whose work at Gallup fundamentally reframed how America evaluates higher education — shifting focus from inputs like selectivity and spending to outputs like engagement, entrepreneurial aspiration, and lifelong thriving. As former Executive Director of Education at Gallup and current Chief Innovation Officer at FIRE, he operates at the intersection of large-scale survey science, workforce development, and campus culture, insisting that the metrics universities optimize for (US News rankings, endowment size, acceptance rates) bear little relationship to what actually predicts graduate success. His central insight challenges the assumption that prestigious degrees guarantee ROI: the Gallup-Purdue Index, surveying over 30,000 college graduates, found that emotional engagement during college — feeling supported by professors, working on long-term projects, completing internships applying classroom learning — predicts workplace thriving and personal wellbeing decades later, while institutional selectivity and GPA show weak to nonexistent correlation with these outcomes.

This finding upended conventional wisdom driving college choice and institutional strategy. Parents and students chase elite brands assuming they confer lasting advantage; universities invest billions in facilities, star faculty, and financial aid to climb rankings. Yet Busteed's data showed that a graduate from a regional state university who had a mentor who cared about them as a person, worked on a semester-long project requiring sustained effort, and completed an internship testing their learning in real-world settings was more likely to be engaged at work and thriving in life ten years later than a disengaged Ivy League graduate with a 3.8 GPA. The implication is radical: access to transformative experiences matters more than access to prestigious names, and institutions should compete on delivering mentorship, experiential learning, and belonging rather than hoarding selectivity. This research catalyzed the "completion agenda" movement emphasizing student success metrics over enrollment numbers, and influenced federal gainful employment rules tying aid eligibility to graduate outcomes rather than institutional reputation.

Busteed's work on entrepreneurial aspiration identified another overlooked predictor: the desire to start a business or lead innovation strongly correlates with post-college engagement regardless of whether graduates actually launch ventures. Students with high entrepreneurial intent — even those who become intrapreneurs within large organizations — show greater initiative, resilience, and satisfaction than those pursuing conventional career paths without creative agency. Yet most universities neither measure nor cultivate this trait, treating entrepreneurship as a niche elective for business majors rather than a mindset applicable across disciplines. Busteed urged institutions to embed entrepreneurial thinking throughout curricula — design challenges in engineering, venture creation in liberal arts, social innovation in public health — recognizing that the aspiration to create value, not just capture credentials, drives the engagement that predicts thriving.

His pivot to FIRE reflects a deepening concern about the campus conditions necessary for these outcomes to flourish. The same Gallup rigor he applied to engagement now measures viewpoint diversity and intellectual freedom: FIRE's Campus Free Speech Reports survey tens of thousands of students, documenting rising self-censorship and ideological homogeneity that undermine the university's core mission of producing independent thinkers. When students fear expressing dissenting views — whether conservative on progressive campuses or progressive on conservative ones — they disengage from the very intellectual risk-taking that education requires. Busteed argues that free speech is not a peripheral culture-war issue but a measurable educational outcome correlated with critical thinking, civic preparedness, and the cognitive flexibility employers demand. His open-source Campus Climate Survey Toolkit enables institutions to diagnose these problems with validated instruments rather than anecdotes, creating accountability for the intellectual environment alongside traditional metrics like graduation rates.

At 50+, Busteed remains focused on the collision between AI and campus discourse. His recent publications show that chatbot-mediated discussions — increasingly used to scale seminar-style learning — reduce viewpoint diversity by 22% compared to human-facilitated conversations, as students defer to algorithmic consensus rather than risking unpopular positions. He warns that efficiency gains from AI tutoring and automated feedback may come at the cost of the messy, uncomfortable, deeply human exchanges where students test beliefs, encounter difference, and develop the intellectual courage that engagement research shows predicts lifelong success. His through-line across two decades: education's value lies not in credentials conferred but in transformations experienced — mentorship that sees the whole person, projects that demand sustained effort, internships that connect learning to purpose, and communities where every voice can be heard without fear — and the path to restoring that value runs through measuring what matters, cultivating entrepreneurial agency, and defending the free exchange of ideas that makes all other learning possible.
