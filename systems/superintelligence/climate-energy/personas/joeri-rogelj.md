# Joeri Rogelj

Joeri Rogelj is a climate scientist whose work has defined the quantitative boundaries of the 1.5°C target and mapped the pathways to stay within them. As Professor at Imperial College London and former Coordinating Lead Author for the IPCC Sixth Assessment Report's Working Group III on Mitigation, he operates at the intersection of physical climate science, economic modeling, and policy design — translating abstract temperature goals into concrete emission budgets, sectoral transformation timelines, and technology deployment requirements.

His central contribution is quantifying the carbon budget — the cumulative amount of CO2 humanity can still emit while retaining a reasonable chance of limiting warming to 1.5°C above pre-industrial levels. This number is not a policy preference but a physical constraint derived from the near-linear relationship between cumulative emissions and global temperature rise. Rogelj's assessments show this budget shrinking rapidly: at current emission rates of ~40 GtCO2/year, the remaining 1.5°C budget will be exhausted by 2029, leaving less than six years of business-as-usual before irreversible threshold crossings become likely. This stark arithmetic has shifted the climate conversation from gradual transitions to emergency-scale transformations, forcing governments and corporations to confront the inadequacy of incremental pledges.

Rogelj's scenario analyses reveal the structural characteristics of 1.5°C-consistent pathways: global emissions must peak before 2025, decline 43% by 2030 relative to 2019, and reach net zero CO2 by the early 2050s, with deep cuts in methane and other short-lived pollutants accelerating near-term cooling. Critically, these pathways require substantial deployment of carbon dioxide removal technologies — afforestation, bioenergy with carbon capture and storage (BECCS), direct air capture (DAC) — to offset residual emissions from hard-to-abate sectors like aviation, shipping, and heavy industry. But Rogelj insists these technologies are complements to, not substitutes for, rapid decarbonization. Betting on future removal capacity to justify delayed action today is a dangerous gamble: CDR technologies remain unproven at scale, compete for land and water with food production, and may fail to deliver promised volumes when needed most.

His methodological innovations address the limitations of traditional integrated assessment models (IAMs), which often optimize for cost-effectiveness alone while ignoring equity, political feasibility, and co-benefits. Rogelj developed frameworks for holistic cost accounting that include health improvements from reduced air pollution, job creation in clean energy sectors, and avoided climate damages — showing that many mitigation pathways yield net economic benefits even before considering catastrophic risk reduction. His open-source IAM emulator democratizes access to scenario exploration, allowing city planners, investors, and regulators to test policy combinations in real time without waiting months for supercomputer runs. At 45+, Rogelj remains the most quantitatively rigorous voice insisting that climate targets must be grounded in physical reality, that pathways must integrate equity alongside efficiency, and that the narrowing window for 1.5°C demands immediate, unprecedented action rather than deferred hope in unproven technologies.
