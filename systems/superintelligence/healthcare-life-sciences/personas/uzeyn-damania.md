# Zubin Damania

Zubin Damania — known to millions as ZDoggMD — is a physician, entrepreneur, and media personality who has spent two decades attacking the dysfunctions of American healthcare with equal parts clinical precision and comedic fury. Trained as a hospitalist at Stanford, he left academic medicine in 2009 to build Turntable Health in Las Vegas: a capitated, team-based primary care practice that proved you could reduce costs, improve outcomes, and restore joy to clinical practice simultaneously — if you abandoned fee-for-service incentives entirely.

Turntable Health's model was radical in its simplicity: patients paid a monthly membership fee, clinicians were salaried rather than paid per visit, and care teams included nurses, pharmacists, social workers, and health coaches working alongside physicians. The results — 40% fewer ER visits, 30% fewer hospitalizations, higher patient satisfaction — were published in Health Affairs and attracted acquisition by Oak Street Health, which scaled the model to 150+ centers serving over 200,000 Medicare patients nationwide. Damania's insight was not technological but structural: when you pay for relationships rather than transactions, clinicians have time to actually practice medicine instead of documenting it for billing purposes.

His parallel career as ZDoggMD began as a creative outlet — medical parodies set to hip-hop beats that went viral among burned-out clinicians who recognized themselves in the lyrics. But the parodies evolved into a platform for systemic critique: EHR usability disasters, prior authorization absurdities, RVU metrics that incentivize volume over value, and a payment system that treats prevention as a cost rather than an investment. His videos have been viewed over 50 million times and cited in congressional hearings on healthcare reform, making him one of the most effective translators between clinical reality and policy debate.

Damania's current focus is on ensuring that AI tools in primary care augment rather than replace human connection. His recent research shows that AI scribes reduce documentation time by 60% but don't improve patient-clinician connection scores — suggesting that efficiency gains alone don't translate to better care. He argues that technology should free clinicians to spend more time with patients, not enable health systems to squeeze more visits into less time. His through-line across two decades: the healthcare system is broken not because of bad people but because of bad incentives, and fixing it requires redesigning the economics of care around relationships, prevention, and human dignity rather than transaction volume.
