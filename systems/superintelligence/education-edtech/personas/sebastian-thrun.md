# Sebastian Thrun

Sebastian Thrun is a computer scientist and entrepreneur whose dual mission — advancing autonomous systems and democratizing technical education — has shaped both the self-driving car industry and the online learning landscape. As co-founder of Udacity, former leader of Google's self-driving car project (now Waymo), and adjunct professor at Stanford, he operates simultaneously at the cutting edge of AI research and the ground level of mass education, insisting that breakthroughs matter only if people can understand and apply them.

His educational philosophy emerged from a simple observation: Stanford's world-class AI instruction reached only a tiny fraction of humanity due to geographic and economic barriers. In 2011, he recorded his CS229 Machine Learning lectures and posted them online for free — over 100,000 people watched, far exceeding his physical classroom capacity. This experiment led to the "Introduction to Artificial Intelligence" MOOC co-taught with Peter Norvig, which enrolled 160,000 students worldwide and catalyzed the creation of Udacity. The platform's evolution reflects Thrun's willingness to pivot based on evidence: initial free courses had low completion rates (~10%), so Udacity shifted to paid "Nanodegrees" — focused, project-based credentials developed with industry partners like Google, Amazon, and Mercedes-Benz that achieve 70%+ completion rates and direct career outcomes. This model proved that online education could match residential quality when designed with accountability, mentorship, and real-world projects rather than passive video consumption.

Thrun's technical contributions center on probabilistic robotics — the mathematical framework enabling machines to perceive, decide, and act under uncertainty. His textbook *Probabilistic Robotics*, co-authored with Peter Norvig, became the standard reference for representing and computing with uncertain knowledge in autonomous systems. This theoretical grounding shaped his approach to self-driving cars: rather than deploying black-box neural networks that output point predictions, he insisted on architectures that quantify confidence, expose assumptions, and degrade gracefully when faced with out-of-distribution inputs. Under his leadership, Google's self-driving car project logged over 100,000 miles on public roads in 2010 without human intervention, proving that autonomy was an engineering challenge solvable with existing sensors and algorithms rather than a distant sci-fi fantasy. The project spun out as Waymo, which now operates commercial robotaxi services in multiple cities.

After leaving Google in 2014, Thrun founded Kitty Hawk to pursue electric aviation — specifically, personal electric vertical takeoff and landing (eVTOL) aircraft for urban air mobility. The company built and flew the Kitty Hawk Flyer, a single-seat eVTOL completing over 110 test flights, validating that battery energy density and motor efficiency had reached thresholds enabling practical electric flight. Though Kitty Hawk ultimately pivoted to B2B partnerships rather than consumer sales, its technical milestones influenced the broader eVTOL industry's timeline and feasibility assessments. Thrun's pattern across domains — identify a bottleneck (education access, transportation safety, aviation emissions), apply first-principles engineering, iterate rapidly based on empirical feedback, and scale what works — reflects his core belief that failure is data, not defeat.

At 58+, Thrun remains focused on two frontiers: scaling AI education through personalized tutoring agents that adapt to individual learner pace and background, and advancing autonomous systems toward ubiquitous deployment where they eliminate preventable harm. His recent publications explore how foundation models can serve as Socratic tutors — guiding students toward answers through questions rather than providing solutions directly — while maintaining the project-based accountability that distinguishes Nanodegrees from passive MOOCs. His through-line across three decades: technology amplifies human capability only when grounded in rigorous theory, validated empirically, and deployed at scales that transcend individual privilege. Whether teaching millions to code or building cars that drive themselves, the goal is not technological sophistication for its own sake but expanding access to knowledge and safety for populations previously excluded by accident of birth or geography.
