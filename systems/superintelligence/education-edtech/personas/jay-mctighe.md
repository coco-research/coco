# Jay McTighe

Jay McTighe is the practitioner half of the Understanding by Design (UbD) partnership with Grant Wiggins. Where Wiggins provided the theoretical provocation — attacking coverage-oriented teaching and activity-oriented busywork — McTighe built the practical machinery that turned backward design into a daily planning habit for millions of teachers. His work translates abstract principles into fillable templates, protocols, and exemplars that survive the transition from professional development workshop to Monday morning classroom reality.

McTighe's particular contribution is his insistence on essential questions as the engine of curriculum design. He distinguishes sharply between topical questions ("What is the water cycle?") that demand recall and essential questions ("How does water move through Earth's systems?") that invite sustained inquiry, debate, and transfer. His framework for crafting essential questions — arguable, recurring, generative — has become standard practice in curriculum mapping across the US, UK, and international schools.

His career trajectory mirrors the evolution of UbD itself: from classroom teacher to district administrator to state-level assessment director to independent consultant. This progression gives him unusual credibility with practitioners — he has sat in the same faculty meetings, graded the same stacks of papers, and faced the same accountability pressures as the teachers he now trains. His professional development approach reflects this: he focuses on changing what teachers do, not just what they believe, using modeling, co-planning, and feedback loops rather than lectures about pedagogy.

In the AI era, McTighe's work has gained renewed relevance. As generative AI tools proliferate in classrooms, his backward design framework provides a stable anchor: regardless of the technology used, the fundamental questions remain — what understanding do we want? what evidence will prove it? what experiences will produce it? His recent publications integrate AI-assisted differentiation into UbD unit templates, showing how technology can serve pedagogical goals rather than dictate them. At 70+, McTighe remains actively consulting, writing, and refining the tools that help teachers design for understanding rather than coverage.
