# Julie Cohen

Julie Cohen is a leading law scholar whose work examines how informational capitalism transforms relationships between individuals, corporations, and the state through pervasive data collection, algorithmic processing, and intellectual property expansion. As Professor at Georgetown University Law Center, she combines critical theory with doctrinal analysis to argue that privacy and copyright are not isolated legal domains but interconnected mechanisms of information control serving corporate interests at the expense of democratic governance and individual autonomy. Her central insight challenges the assumption that data extraction reflects voluntary exchange: when platforms design interfaces to maximize disclosure while obscuring downstream uses, user "consent" masks structural power imbalances that prevent meaningful choice.

Her book "Between Truth and Power" traced the historical evolution from broadcast media's relatively contained data flows to platform capitalism's comprehensive surveillance infrastructure. Where television networks collected audience ratings through sampling, digital platforms track every click, scroll, pause, and interaction, creating detailed behavioral profiles used for prediction and modification. Cohen demonstrates that this transformation was not technologically inevitable but legally constructed through favorable interpretations of contract law, weak enforcement of unfair competition statutes, and legislative capture preventing meaningful privacy regulation. The result is an economy where human experience itself becomes raw material for extraction, processed through proprietary algorithms to generate predictions sold to advertisers, insurers, employers, and governments.

On privacy theory, Cohen critiques the "privacy paradox" narrative suggesting users irrationally claim to value privacy while disclosing freely online. This framing misunderstands both the design of digital environments and the nature of autonomous choice. Platforms employ dark patterns — interface designs exploiting cognitive biases — to encourage maximum disclosure while burying privacy settings behind multiple menus. Even informed users face collective action problems: refusing one platform's terms may mean exclusion from essential social and economic networks, making exit impractical regardless of privacy preferences. Cohen argues for substantive limits on data processing that do not depend on individual vigilance, similar to how consumer protection law prohibits unfair contract terms regardless of whether consumers read them.

Cohen's copyright scholarship connects these themes by showing how intellectual property expansion reinforces surveillance infrastructure. Digital rights management systems required by DMCA anti-circumvention provisions create technical barriers preventing lawful uses like fair dealing, first sale, and preservation. Meanwhile, copyright term extensions have created de facto perpetual monopolies contrary to the Constitution's "limited times" requirement, transferring wealth from the public domain to corporate rights holders while providing minimal additional incentive for creation. Her analysis influenced academic challenges to copyright overreach and supported legislative proposals restoring balance through shorter terms and stronger user rights.

At 55+, Cohen remains focused on emerging challenges in AI and biometric surveillance: when machine learning models infer sensitive attributes from seemingly innocuous data, traditional privacy frameworks fail because individuals cannot anticipate what will be deduced about them. Facial recognition technology enables unprecedented tracking in public spaces, chilling expressive activities essential to democratic participation. Cohen advocates for comprehensive bans on certain surveillance applications combined with affirmative obligations for algorithmic transparency and impact assessments. Through three decades of scholarship, her message remains consistent: information law succeeds when it serves democratic values of autonomy, equality, and self-governance rather than treating human beings as mere data sources to be mined and manipulated for corporate profit.
