# Dan Burk

Dan Burk is a leading patent law scholar whose work examines how intellectual property systems shape innovation incentives across biotechnology, software, and emerging technologies. As Professor at University of California Irvine School of Law, he combines doctrinal analysis with economic theory and empirical evidence to argue for patent policies calibrated to industry-specific characteristics rather than uniform rules applied across all sectors. His central insight challenges the assumption that stronger patent protection always promotes more innovation: when patents create thickets of overlapping rights or lock up basic research tools, they can impede the cumulative innovation process that drives technological progress.

His research on biotechnology patents demonstrates the tension between incentivizing drug development and maintaining access to research tools: when companies patent genes, proteins, or laboratory techniques used by academic researchers, downstream innovation requires negotiating multiple licenses that increase transaction costs and delay discoveries. Burk argues for robust research exemptions allowing non-commercial use of patented inventions without permission, ensuring that basic science remains accessible even when commercial applications require licensing. His analysis influenced the Supreme Court's decision in Association for Molecular Pathology v. Myriad Genetics, which invalidated patents on naturally occurring gene sequences while preserving protection for engineered diagnostic methods.

On software patents, Burk traces the doctrinal confusion following the Supreme Court's Alice decision invalidating patents on abstract ideas implemented on generic computers. Lower courts struggled to distinguish eligible software inventions from ineligible abstract concepts, creating uncertainty that discouraged investment in some areas while enabling opportunistic litigation in others. Burk proposes legislative clarification establishing that software claiming improvements to computer functionality itself (faster processing, better security, more efficient storage) deserves patent protection, while software merely automating business processes using conventional computing does not constitute patentable subject matter. This functional distinction provides clearer guidance than the current two-step test that leaves examiners and judges guessing.

Burk's work on trade secret expansion documents how companies increasingly rely on confidentiality rather than patent disclosure to protect innovations, driven by concerns about prior art searches, examination quality, and foreign enforcement. While trade secrets avoid these problems, they create information asymmetries: competitors cannot design around unknown technologies, employees face mobility restrictions preventing them from applying their skills elsewhere, and reverse engineering prohibitions limit the competitive pressures that drive improvement. The Defend Trade Secrets Act provided federal remedies but did not address these systemic concerns about reduced knowledge dissemination.

At 60+, Burk remains focused on AI-generated inventions where traditional inventorship requirements face unprecedented challenges: when machine learning systems produce novel solutions without human direction, who qualifies as the inventor — the programmer, the user providing training data, or is the invention unpatentable for lack of human conception? Current law requires human inventors, but Burk argues that policy considerations may warrant sui generis protection for AI-generated innovations to maintain investment incentives while acknowledging that human contribution may be minimal. Through three decades of scholarship, his message remains consistent: patent law succeeds when it precisely targets market failures in innovation financing without creating broader monopolies than necessary, and that precision requires continuous calibration based on empirical evidence about what actually promotes technological progress rather than theoretical assumptions about incentive structures.
