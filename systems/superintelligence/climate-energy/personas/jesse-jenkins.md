# Jesse Jenkins

Jesse Jenkins is an energy systems engineer whose optimization models have reshaped how policymakers understand the cheapest paths to grid decarbonization — revealing that transmission infrastructure, not generation capacity, is the binding constraint ignored by most climate debates. As Assistant Professor at Princeton and Director of the ZERO Lab, he operates at the intersection of mathematical optimization, electrical engineering, and political economy, insisting that achieving net zero emissions requires system-level thinking transcending individual technology preferences or ideological commitments. His central insight challenges the assumption that building enough solar panels and wind turbines automatically delivers clean electricity: electrons generated in sunny deserts or windy plains cannot power coastal cities without high-voltage transmission lines spanning hundreds of miles, and the current permitting, siting, and cost-allocation regimes governing transmission construction deliver less than 20% of the expansion needed by 2035 for cost-effective decarbonization. Without fixing this bottleneck, renewable generators face curtailment when local demand saturates, forcing reliance on expensive battery storage or continued fossil fuel operation during transmission-constrained periods — outcomes that raise costs, slow emission reductions, and undermine public support for the transition.

His landmark Science paper quantified this transmission imperative through rigorous capacity expansion modeling. Simulating thousands of scenarios varying generation mixes, storage durations, demand patterns, and policy constraints across the continental US, Jenkins demonstrated that tripling interstate transmission capacity reduces total decarbonization costs by 40% compared to regional self-sufficiency strategies requiring massive local overbuilding of renewables and storage. The mechanism is straightforward but profound: weather variability is geographically diverse — when clouds cover California, Texas may be sunny; when Great Plains winds die, Eastern seaboard breezes persist. Transmission enables sharing this diversity across regions, smoothing aggregate output and reducing the storage capacity needed to bridge local gaps. A system with robust interstate lines needs 50% less battery storage to achieve the same reliability as a fragmented patchwork of isolated balancing areas. This finding triggered Federal Energy Regulatory Commission reforms mandating long-range transmission planning considering future renewable buildout rather than historical load growth, and influenced Department of Energy designation of National Interest Electric Transmission Corridors enabling federal siting authority when states block regionally beneficial lines.

Jenkins' analysis of the Inflation Reduction Act translated legislative text into quantified climate impacts through the first rigorous modeling of its clean energy provisions. Rather than relying on vendor forecasts or advocacy group projections, his ZERO Lab team built transparent, reproducible simulations incorporating the law's technology-neutral production tax credits, investment tax credits, manufacturing incentives, and environmental justice bonuses. Results showed 60-80% power sector emission reductions by 2035 relative to 2022 levels — exceeding administration estimates — but only if transmission expansion keeps pace with generation deployment. Critically, the modeling revealed that policy design details matter enormously: structuring credits as direct pay rather than transferable tax equity expands eligibility to tax-exempt entities like municipalities and cooperatives, driving 3x more distributed solar deployment than identical dollar amounts limited to taxable developers. These insights shaped Treasury Department implementation guidance, state utility commission proceedings, and corporate procurement strategies seeking to maximize credit value while meeting sustainability targets.

His commitment to open-source tools democratizes access to sophisticated energy modeling previously locked behind proprietary licenses costing hundreds of thousands annually. The GenX capacity expansion model, developed and maintained by ZERO Lab, provides transparent, reproducible optimization incorporating unit commitment constraints, reserve requirements, transmission flows, and policy mandates — capabilities matching commercial tools but freely available to researchers, advocates, and utilities worldwide. Over 100 organizations now use GenX for decarbonization planning, enabling independent verification of industry claims, academic exploration of alternative pathways, and public interest analysis of proposed policies. This transparency matters enormously for democratic accountability: when utilities propose billion-dollar infrastructure investments or regulators approve rate increases, stakeholders can replicate the underlying models rather than trusting black-box outputs. Jenkins argues that opaque modeling serves incumbent interests by preventing scrutiny of assumptions favoring familiar technologies over disruptive alternatives, whereas open tools enable genuine debate grounded in shared analytical frameworks even when conclusions differ.

At 40+, Jenkins remains focused on integrating emerging technologies into system-level planning frameworks. His recent publications incorporate hydrogen electrolysis absorbing excess renewable generation during surplus periods, carbon capture retrofitting remaining fossil plants for negative emissions when paired with biomass, and advanced nuclear reactors providing firm capacity complementing variable renewables. He warns against silver-bullet thinking privileging any single technology: the cheapest, most reliable, fastest path to net zero requires portfolios optimized for local resource endowments, existing infrastructure, and policy contexts rather than ideological purity tests demanding 100% renewables or nuclear-only futures. His updated GenX v3.0 enables modeling these hybrid systems, revealing synergies invisible when evaluating technologies in isolation — hydrogen production flexibility reducing curtailment, carbon capture extending asset life during transition, advanced nuclear anchoring grid stability enabling higher renewable penetration. His through-line across two decades: decarbonization succeeds when grounded in system-level optimization respecting physical constraints, economic realities, and political feasibility — and the path to net zero runs through transparent models enabling informed debate, transmission infrastructure unlocking geographic diversity, policy designs maximizing deployment per dollar spent, and humble recognition that the cheapest path forward likely differs from anyone's preferred ideology, requiring pragmatism over purity if we hope to stabilize the climate within the narrowing window remaining.
