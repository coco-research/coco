# Margaret Hamburg

Margaret Hamburg is a physician and public health leader who has governed at the intersection of science, regulation, and politics during some of the most challenging health crises of the past three decades. As FDA Commissioner (2009-2015), she oversaw the implementation of the Food Safety Modernization Act, launched the Sentinel Initiative for real-time drug safety monitoring across 300 million patients, and navigated the contentious debates around accelerated drug approvals, generic drug shortages, and tobacco regulation. Her tenure was defined by a commitment to "regulatory science" — using rigorous data and transparent processes to make decisions that balance innovation speed with patient safety.

Before the FDA, Hamburg served as New York City's Health Commissioner during the height of the AIDS epidemic and a resurgent tuberculosis crisis. She expanded needle exchange programs despite political opposition, implemented directly observed therapy for TB that reversed rising infection rates, and built coalitions across fractured communities to deliver public health interventions. This experience forged her belief that public health is fundamentally about trust — trust that erodes quickly when institutions are opaque or inconsistent, and that must be actively maintained through transparency about uncertainty and tradeoffs.

Her post-FDA career has focused on global health security. As Senior Fellow at the Council on Foreign Relations and former President of AAAS, she has advocated for sustained investment in pandemic preparedness — not just emergency funding during outbreaks but permanent surveillance infrastructure, laboratory networks, and workforce development. Her work on the Global Health Security Agenda helped establish a framework adopted by over 70 nations to strengthen detection, response, and prevention capacities. She argues that pathogens don't respect borders, and neither should our governance systems — a lesson reinforced by COVID-19 but often forgotten once the acute crisis passes.

At 70+, Hamburg remains active in shaping how emerging technologies — particularly AI-based diagnostics and therapeutics — are regulated. Her recent publications propose adaptive approval frameworks that allow iterative evaluation of learning algorithms while maintaining safety standards. She insists that innovation and regulation are not opposites but partners: guardrails that make new tools trustworthy enough to scale widely. Her through-line across four decades of public service: public health works best when it is invisible, prevents catastrophes before they happen, and maintains credibility through honesty about what we know, what we don't, and how we decide.
