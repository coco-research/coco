# Paul Farmer

Paul Farmer (1959–2022) was a physician, anthropologist, and co-founder of Partners In Health who proved that world-class medical care is deliverable in the poorest communities on Earth — not as an aspiration but as a demonstrated fact. His work in rural Haiti, Lima's shantytowns, and post-genocide Rwanda overturned decades of conventional wisdom that said complex chronic disease treatment (HIV, multidrug-resistant TB, cancer) required advanced infrastructure unavailable in low-resource settings.

Farmer's central concept — structural violence — named the mechanism by which social inequality becomes biological outcome. Poverty, racism, gender discrimination, and political marginalization don't just correlate with poor health; they actively produce it by constraining access to care, nutrition, clean water, and safety. Medicine that treats pathogens while ignoring these forces is, in Farmer's view, complicit in the violence it claims to oppose. His book *Pathologies of Power* made this argument with clinical precision and moral fury, becoming a foundational text in global health ethics.

His practical methodology — accompaniment — meant walking with patients through the entire course of illness rather than prescribing and departing. In Haiti, this meant community health workers delivering antiretrovirals door-to-door, providing food support, transportation vouchers, and housing repairs alongside medical treatment. The results were staggering: HIV viral suppression rates matching those in Boston, MDR-TB cure rates exceeding 80% in Lima's poorest neighborhoods. These outcomes forced WHO, PEPFAR, and the Global Fund to abandon their position that such treatment was infeasible outside wealthy settings — directly enabling the scale-up that has saved over 50 million lives.

Farmer died suddenly in Rwanda in 2022 at age 62, where he was teaching and treating patients at the University of Global Health Equity he had helped build. His legacy is not just the hospitals, clinics, and trained workforce he left behind, but a permanent shift in what global health considers possible. Every program that delivers cancer chemotherapy in rural Africa, dialysis in post-earthquake Haiti, or mental health services in refugee camps stands on ground Farmer cleared. His insistence that the barrier was never feasibility but will remains the most powerful challenge to anyone who says world-class care cannot reach the world's poorest.
