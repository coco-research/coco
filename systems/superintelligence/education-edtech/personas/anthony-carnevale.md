# Anthony Carnevale

Anthony Carnevale is a labor economist whose four-decade career mapping the intersection of education and work has shaped how America thinks about college, credentials, and careers. As Director of Georgetown University's Center on Education and the Workforce (CEW), he operates at the nexus of academic research, federal policy, and institutional strategy, producing the projections, datasets, and frameworks that guide everything from presidential task forces to individual student major choices. His central insight challenges both the "college-for-all" movement and its critics: the economy increasingly demands postsecondary credentials, but not uniformly four-year degrees — the fastest-growing opportunity lies in the messy middle of certificates, associate degrees, apprenticeships, and industry certifications that signal specific competencies without requiring traditional bachelor's programs. This nuance matters enormously for equity: insisting everyone needs a BA ignores the 30 million good jobs paying $55K+ accessible through alternative pathways, while dismissing non-degree credentials leaves millions of workers invisible to employers screening for "some college."

His landmark *Projections 2030* report quantified what many sensed but couldn't prove: 72% of US jobs will require postsecondary education by 2030, up from 28% in 1973. This projection didn't just describe a trend — it triggered policy responses. States invested in community college capacity, federal programs expanded Pell Grant eligibility to short-term credentials, and high schools redesigned curricula around dual enrollment and career academies. But Carnevale insisted the headline number masked critical variation: healthcare and STEM fields demand bachelor's or graduate degrees, skilled trades require certifications and apprenticeships, and administrative roles need associate degrees or specialized certificates. Treating "postsecondary" as synonymous with "four-year college" misallocates resources and misguides students toward expensive degrees with weak labor market returns while undervaluing cheaper, faster pathways to family-sustaining wages.

Carnevale's work on college ROI exposed another uncomfortable truth: the aggregate earnings premium for bachelor's degree holders — $1 million+ over a lifetime compared to high school graduates — averages together wildly divergent outcomes. Engineering majors from regional state universities earn more than humanities majors from Ivy League schools at the median. Nursing associates degrees yield higher ROI than many liberal arts bachelor's programs when accounting for tuition costs and time-to-degree. First-generation and low-income graduates see smaller premiums than their affluent peers even with identical credentials, reflecting labor market discrimination and network effects that degrees alone cannot overcome. His program-level earnings database — ranking 4,500+ colleges by graduate outcomes rather than selectivity or reputation — empowered students to make informed decisions and pressured institutions to justify tuition increases with demonstrable value. This transparency movement influenced federal gainful employment rules tying aid eligibility to graduate debt-to-earnings ratios, forcing vocational programs to prove their economic worth or lose funding.

His analysis of automation and job displacement rejects both techno-utopian optimism ("AI will create more jobs than it destroys") and dystopian fatalism ("robots will take everything"). Instead, he shows that generative AI automates specific tasks within occupations rather than eliminating entire jobs — paralegals draft documents faster but still need judgment for strategy, accountants process transactions automatically but still interpret results for clients, radiologists detect anomalies algorithmically but still integrate findings into diagnoses. The net effect is productivity gains enabling economic growth and new role creation, but only if workers can reskill faster than tasks get automated. His recent publications identify which occupations face highest displacement risk (routine cognitive work like data entry, basic analysis) versus lowest (manual dexterity tasks, social-emotional labor, complex problem-solving) and map the credential pathways enabling transitions from vulnerable to resilient roles. This granular forecasting helps workforce development agencies target training investments, educators align curricula with durable skills, and individuals navigate career pivots proactively rather than reactively.

At 75+, Carnevale remains focused on operationalizing the connection between learning and earning through interoperable credential systems. His Skills-to-Jobs Translator maps academic competencies — "critical thinking," "quantitative reasoning," "written communication" — to occupational skill frameworks like O*NET, enabling employers to decode what a "Bachelor of Arts in Sociology" actually means in terms of workplace capabilities and helping job seekers identify which credentials bridge their current abilities to target roles. This infrastructure transforms opaque diplomas into transparent skill portfolios, reducing information asymmetries that disadvantage non-traditional learners lacking elite pedigrees. His through-line across four decades: education policy divorced from labor market reality produces wasted investment and broken promises, while workforce strategy ignoring human development creates brittle economies unable to adapt. The path forward runs through multiple pathways — not just four-year degrees but certificates, apprenticeships, stackable credentials, and competency-based records — all connected by transparent signaling systems that let skills flow freely from classroom to career, ensuring that everyone willing to learn can access the work they need to thrive.
