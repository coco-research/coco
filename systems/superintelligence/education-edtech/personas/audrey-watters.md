# Audrey Watters

Audrey Watters is a journalist, historian, and independent researcher who has spent over a decade exposing the recurring myths, hidden politics, and extractive economics of educational technology. Her work rejects the dominant narrative that EdTech represents linear progress toward better learning; instead, she demonstrates that today's adaptive algorithms, learning analytics, and AI tutors are the latest iterations of a century-old dream: automating instruction to reduce costs, standardize outcomes, and shift power from teachers to administrators and vendors. Her book *Teaching Machines* traces this lineage from Sidney Pressey's mechanical testing devices in the 1920s through B.F. Skinner's programmed instruction in the 1950s to contemporary personalized learning platforms, showing how each wave promised liberation but delivered surveillance and control.

Watters' methodology combines historical rigor with sharp political critique. She refuses to evaluate technologies solely on their stated pedagogical goals, insisting instead on asking who profits, who loses autonomy, and what ideologies are encoded in the software's design. Her "Monsters of Education Technology" keynote series — delivered annually from 2014 to 2021 — identified emerging threats before they became mainstream: blockchain credentials that fragment transcripts into tradeable tokens, emotion recognition AI that pathologizes student affect, and proctoring software that normalizes constant surveillance as a condition of assessment. By framing these trends as "monsters," she drew on folklore traditions to make abstract risks visceral and memorable, warning educators that the scariest things in EdTech are not glitches but features working exactly as designed.

Her investigative reporting on data privacy revealed how student information systems, learning management platforms, and classroom apps aggregate behavioral data into predictive models that forecast dropout risk, college readiness, and even criminal propensity. These inferences — often opaque to students, parents, and teachers — shape tracking decisions, intervention allocations, and disciplinary actions long before any human reviews them. Watters argues that privacy laws focusing on data collection miss the point: the harm lies in the inferences drawn from aggregated patterns, which encode historical biases into seemingly objective scores. Her proposed solution is not better consent forms but structural resistance: open infrastructure owned by schools, collective bargaining by educators over tool selection, and contractual prohibitions on behavioral inference and resale.

After closing her Hack Education blog in 2021 — citing burnout from fighting alone against well-funded industry narratives — Watters shifted to book projects, speaking engagements, and toolkit development. Her recent work addresses generative AI in education, arguing that LLM-based tutors replicate Skinnerian behaviorism at unprecedented scale: shaping student responses through immediate feedback loops without human judgment, contextual understanding, or ethical oversight. She warns that the efficiency gains promised by AI automation come at the cost of relational pedagogy — the messy, improvisational, deeply human work of teaching that no algorithm can replicate. At 50+, Watters remains the most historically grounded voice insisting that educational technology must be judged not by its novelty but by its politics, and that the future of learning depends on whether we build tools that serve teachers and students or systems that replace them with cheaper, more controllable substitutes.
