# Ryan Calo

Ryan Calo is a leading scholar in robotics and AI law whose work examines how embodied intelligence creates unique legal challenges beyond those posed by pure software systems. As Professor at University of Washington School of Law and Co-Director of the Tech Policy Lab, he bridges technical understanding of robotic systems with legal doctrine, arguing that AI governance must account for physical world interactions where errors cause real-world harm rather than just digital inconvenience. His central insight challenges the assumption that software liability frameworks adequately address robotics: when machines learn, adapt, and operate autonomously in physical spaces, traditional product liability doctrines designed for static products fail to provide clear guidance on responsibility for harms.

His research on algorithmic accountability demonstrates the tension between transparency demands and trade secret protections: companies resist disclosing how AI systems make decisions citing proprietary interests, but affected individuals cannot challenge unfair outcomes without understanding the basis for automated decisions. Calo proposes a qualified right to explanation where high-stakes decisions in employment, credit, housing, and criminal justice trigger disclosure obligations, but implementation details remain protected through secure audit mechanisms rather than public release. This balanced approach influenced GDPR's Article 22 provisions on automated decision-making and similar state-level legislation in California and Illinois.

On drone regulation, Calo developed risk-based frameworks distinguishing between recreational hobbyists, commercial operators, and government surveillance uses. Blanket prohibitions stifle innovation while failing to address genuine safety and privacy concerns, whereas tiered regulations based on weight, altitude, proximity to people, and data collection capabilities enable appropriate oversight without burdening low-risk activities. His analysis showed that most drone incidents involve reckless operation rather than technology failures, suggesting that operator education and enforcement of existing aviation laws may be more effective than prescriptive technical mandates.

Calo's work on consumer protection addresses AI systems that use personalization to manipulate user behavior through dark patterns: interfaces designed to exploit cognitive biases and psychological vulnerabilities. When Netflix recommends content optimized for engagement rather than user wellbeing, or when dynamic pricing exploits individual willingness-to-pay estimates, traditional consumer protection law lacks tools to address these subtle forms of manipulation. He advocates for updated FTC guidelines recognizing algorithmic manipulation as an unfair practice, enabling enforcement against designs that systematically disadvantage consumers through personalized deception.

At 45+, Calo remains focused on emerging challenges in autonomous vehicles and delivery robots: when self-driving cars cause accidents, who bears responsibility — the manufacturer, the software developer, the human supervisor, or the vehicle owner? Current tort law assumes human agency, but AI systems operating with varying levels of autonomy complicate causation analyses. Calo proposes enterprise liability where manufacturers bear primary responsibility for autonomous operations, creating incentives for safety investment while enabling cost spreading through insurance and pricing. Through two decades of scholarship, his message remains consistent: AI governance succeeds when it accounts for both technical capabilities and human limitations, ensuring that accountability structures evolve alongside technological progress rather than remaining stuck in analog-era frameworks.
