# Kate Crawford

Kate Crawford is a leading critical AI scholar whose work exposes the material foundations, power structures, and social costs underlying artificial intelligence systems. As Research Professor at University of Southern California and Co-Founder of the AI Now Institute, she challenges dominant narratives that frame AI as neutral technology or inevitable progress, arguing instead that these are socio-technical systems embedded in extractive economic relationships and unequal power dynamics. Her central insight reframes AI from abstract algorithms to embodied infrastructure: training large language models requires vast computational resources powered by electricity from fossil fuels, datasets built on exploited labor from content moderators and data annotators, and minerals extracted through environmentally destructive mining operations disproportionately affecting indigenous communities.

Her book "Atlas of AI" traced these connections across global supply chains: lithium extraction in Chile's Atacama Desert depletes water tables affecting local agriculture; rare earth mineral processing in Inner Mongolia creates toxic waste streams; data center construction in rural communities increases energy demand while providing limited local employment; content moderation in Nairobi exposes workers to psychological trauma from reviewing violent material. Crawford demonstrates that the "cloud" metaphor deliberately obscures these material realities, allowing tech companies to present AI as weightless and ethically neutral when in fact it rests on concrete physical infrastructure with measurable environmental and social costs. This analysis challenges both techno-utopian promises of AI solving climate change and corporate claims that computational expansion is inherently beneficial.

On algorithmic bias, Crawford critiques narrow technical approaches that treat bias as a statistical error correctable through better data or fairer algorithms. Historical data encodes structural inequalities in housing, employment, criminal justice, and healthcare — training on this data reproduces and amplifies these patterns regardless of technical sophistication. When COMPAS recidivism algorithms produce racially disparate outcomes, or when hiring algorithms penalize women, these are not bugs but features of systems designed to optimize efficiency using data reflecting existing discrimination. Debiasing requires addressing root causes of inequality rather than adjusting model parameters, yet most AI ethics initiatives focus on technical fixes that leave structural problems untouched.

Crawford's advocacy for moratoriums on certain AI applications reflects her assessment that current deployment practices systematically harm marginalized communities while concentrating power in unaccountable corporations. Facial recognition technology exhibits higher error rates for people of color and women, yet continues to be deployed in law enforcement despite documented cases of wrongful arrest. Predictive policing algorithms reinforce over-policing in already-surveilled communities without reducing crime. Her testimony before legislative bodies has emphasized that democratic oversight requires more than transparency reports — it requires substantive constraints on what applications are permissible regardless of technical feasibility.

At 50+, Crawford remains focused on generative AI's impacts on creative labor and intellectual property: when large language models train on copyrighted works without compensation or consent, they externalize costs onto artists, writers, and musicians whose labor produced the cultural commons these models exploit. The resulting systems can replicate styles and generate derivative works that compete with original creators while providing no attribution or remuneration. Crawford argues that current copyright frameworks inadequately address these harms because they were designed for human-to-human copying rather than machine learning on massive scales. Through two decades of scholarship, her message remains consistent: AI governance must account for the full lifecycle of these systems from mineral extraction to disposal, recognizing that technological "progress" that concentrates benefits while distributing costs is not progress at all but rather old forms of exploitation dressed in new computational clothing.
