# Katharine Hayhoe

Katharine Hayhoe is an atmospheric scientist and evangelical Christian who has spent two decades building bridges between climate science and communities that distrust it — particularly conservative, rural, and faith-based audiences. Her central insight challenges the dominant model of science communication: facts alone do not change minds. When people reject climate science, they are often rejecting perceived threats to their identity, values, or tribe — not the data itself. Effective engagement, she argues, starts with shared values (stewardship, security, prosperity, love of place), listens before lecturing, and connects before correcting. Only after trust is established can scientific evidence land without triggering defensive rejection.

Her technical work focuses on regional climate downscaling — translating coarse global model outputs into high-resolution projections that local decision-makers can actually use. A farmer in West Texas does not care about global mean temperature; they care about whether drought severity will increase, when planting windows will shift, and whether irrigation infrastructure will survive projected heat extremes. Hayhoe's Climate Science Center at Texas Tech produces downscaled projections validated against local observations, enabling water managers, agricultural extension agents, and emergency responders to prepare for specific, measurable risks rather than abstract global trends. This localization makes climate change tangible and actionable, bypassing ideological debates about whether it is "real" by focusing on what to do about observable changes already underway.

Hayhoe's unique position as both a rigorous scientist and a committed evangelical allows her to speak authentically to audiences that secular environmentalists cannot reach. She frames climate action as theological stewardship — caring for God's creation, protecting the vulnerable, and exercising responsible dominion over natural resources. Her faith-based climate engagement framework has been adopted by over 10,000 congregations across denominations, translating biblical imperatives into concrete actions like energy efficiency retrofits, sustainable agriculture practices, and advocacy for clean energy policies. This approach does not require abandoning scientific rigor; instead, it embeds science within a moral narrative that resonates with communities where purely technocratic arguments fail.

Her public communication strategy — exemplified by her PBS series *Global Weirding* and her book *Saving Us* — prioritizes empathy over expertise. She answers real questions from real people ("Is it too late?" "What can I actually do?" "Why should I care if I won't see the worst impacts?") with patience, humor, and personal vulnerability. She shares her own doubts, fears, and struggles as a parent facing an uncertain future, making space for others to express theirs without judgment. This relational approach has made her one of the most trusted voices on climate among audiences that typically dismiss environmental messaging as elitist or alarmist.

At 50+, Hayhoe remains focused on scaling what works: training thousands of "climate communicators" in her value-first methodology, producing localized impact data that bypasses polarization, and building coalitions across unlikely alliances — ranchers and environmentalists, pastors and scientists, Republicans and Democrats — united by shared love of place and concern for future generations. Her through-line across two decades: the climate crisis is ultimately a human crisis of connection, and solving it requires rebuilding the trust and common purpose that politics has fractured. Science provides the map, but only shared values can motivate the journey.
