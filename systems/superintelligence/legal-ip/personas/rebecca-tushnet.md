# Rebecca Tushnet

Rebecca Tushnet is a leading copyright and trademark scholar whose work examines how intellectual property law intersects with First Amendment values, often creating tensions between exclusive rights and expressive freedom. As Professor at Harvard Law School, she combines doctrinal precision with critical theory to argue for IP frameworks that serve democratic culture rather than merely protecting corporate interests. Her central insight challenges the assumption that stronger IP protection always promotes creativity: when copyright terms extend far beyond authors' lifetimes or when trademark law controls artistic expression, these expansions stifle the very creative processes they were designed to encourage.

Her research on copyright convergence demonstrates how copyright and trademark doctrines increasingly overlap in problematic ways: companies use trademark law to protect expired copyrighted works, creating de facto perpetual monopolies contrary to copyright's limited duration principle. When Disney uses trademark law to prevent copying of Steamboat Willie after copyright expiration, it undermines the constitutional bargain where works enter public domain after limited exclusivity. Tushnet argues for maintaining clear boundaries between these IP regimes, ensuring that trademark protects source identification while copyright's expiration genuinely frees works for public use.

On fair use, Tushnet recognizes that this doctrine provides essential breathing space for creativity but its case-by-case nature creates uncertainty chilling legitimate uses. Creators cannot afford to litigate even strong fair use claims, so they self-censor rather than risk lawsuits. She advocates for clearer safe harbors for transformative uses where new works add different purpose, meaning, or message regardless of commercial nature. Her defense of fan fiction as critical commentary challenged industry assumptions that all unauthorized uses harm markets, showing instead that fan communities engage in meaningful cultural dialogue that enriches rather than diminishes original works' value.

Tushnet's work on false advertising litigation examines how Lanham Act Section 43(a) provides competitors with tools to challenge deceptive marketing but warns that overly permissive standing rules enable anti-competitive lawsuits. After POM Wonderful v. Coca-Cola allowed Lanham Act claims alongside FDA regulation, lower courts struggled to distinguish genuine deception from mere puffery. Tushnet proposes requiring rigorous proof of material deception affecting purchasing decisions rather than accepting consumer surveys showing abstract confusion about sponsorship relationships. This approach would prevent litigation abuse while maintaining important consumer protection functions.

At 55+, Tushnet remains focused on emerging challenges in AI-generated works: when machine learning systems produce creative outputs, who qualifies as author under copyright law — the programmer, the user providing prompts, or is the work uncopyrightable for lack of human authorship? Current doctrine requires human creativity, but Tushnet argues that policy considerations may warrant sui generis protection for AI-assisted works while maintaining that purely AI-generated outputs should remain in public domain. Through two decades of scholarship, her message remains consistent: intellectual property law succeeds when it serves democratic values of expressive freedom, cultural participation, and creative building upon existing works rather than treating IP as absolute property right disconnected from its constitutional purposes.
