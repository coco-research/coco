# Herbert Hovenkamp

Herbert Hovenkamp is the preeminent antitrust scholar whose treatise has shaped competition law doctrine for decades — providing the authoritative reference that courts cite when determining whether conduct violates the Sherman or Clayton Acts. As Professor at University of Pennsylvania Law School, he combines deep historical knowledge with rigorous economic analysis to argue for antitrust enforcement that protects competition as a process rather than individual competitors, while recognizing that process requires structural safeguards against excessive concentration.

His central contribution challenges both naive populism and extreme Chicago School positions: antitrust law should focus on protecting the competitive process itself, which sometimes requires intervention even when short-term prices remain low. Hovenkamp demonstrates through exhaustive historical analysis that periods of weak enforcement correlate with reduced startup formation, slower productivity growth, and diminished innovation — outcomes that matter more than immediate price effects. His treatise, cited by federal courts over 500 times, provides the definitive statement of antitrust principles that balances theoretical rigor with practical enforceability.

Hovenkamp's work on predatory pricing challenged the dominant Areeda-Turner test, showing that its assumption of rational recoupment fails in markets where network effects and data advantages create durable monopolies. When Amazon sells below cost to gain market share, traditional analysis assumes this cannot be sustained — but Hovenkamp points out that cross-subsidization from profitable segments and investor tolerance for losses change the calculus. His framework considers whether below-cost pricing creates barriers that prevent equally efficient competitors from surviving, regardless of whether recoupment follows the traditional pattern.

On mergers, Hovenkamp advocates returning to structural presumptions: horizontal combinations in already-concentrated markets should face heightened scrutiny because they reduce the number of independent decision-makers and facilitate coordinated behavior. The Chicago School argued that efficiency gains could justify almost any merger, but Hovenkamp shows that claimed efficiencies rarely materialize post-merger while anti-competitive effects are predictable and persistent. In digital markets, his analysis focuses on how acquiring nascent competitors eliminates future competitive threats even when target companies have minimal current revenue — Facebook's Instagram acquisition being the paradigmatic example.

At 75+, Hovenkamp remains focused on adapting antitrust doctrine for AI foundation models, where concentration risks mirror earlier industrial consolidations. Training costs, data requirements, and talent scarcity create barriers that could entrench a handful of providers unless enforcement prevents further consolidation. His recent testimony before Congress advocated for lower notification thresholds for tech acquisitions and restored structural presumptions based on market share and concentration metrics. Through four decades of scholarship, his message remains consistent: competition policy succeeds when it protects the process of rivalry itself, ensuring markets remain open to new entrants who drive innovation and challenge incumbents — because without that structural protection, even vigorous enforcement cannot replicate what competitive markets produce naturally.
