# Paul Goldstein

Paul Goldstein is a leading copyright scholar whose work examines the theoretical foundations and practical applications of intellectual property law across domestic and international contexts. As Professor at University of Southern California Gould School of Law, he combines philosophical rigor with comparative law analysis to argue for copyright frameworks that maintain constitutional balance between creator incentives and public access. His central insight challenges both absolutist property claims and anti-copyright populism: copyright serves important social functions by encouraging creative production, but those functions require carefully calibrated limits preventing monopolies that stifle the very progress copyright was designed to promote.

His comprehensive treatise on international copyright demonstrates how TRIPS Agreement established minimum global standards but left critical gaps in enforcement mechanisms and developing country accommodations. When wealthy nations demand stronger protections through bilateral trade agreements containing TRIPS-plus provisions, they undermine the balance struck by multilateral negotiations. Goldstein argues for genuine harmonization respecting developmental differences rather than one-size-fits-all mandates that privilege rights holders in advanced economies over access needs in emerging markets. His analysis influenced academic critiques of ACTA and TPP intellectual property chapters.

On fair use, Goldstein critiques the Federal Circuit's overemphasis on market harm as determinative of fairness, showing that this approach conflates the existence of licensing markets with actual substitution effects. When courts find fair use lacking simply because rights holders could have licensed the use, they transform fair use from a user right into a compulsory licensing scheme subject to copyright holder veto. Goldstein proposes focusing on whether the secondary use serves a different purpose than the original, ensuring that transformative works adding new meaning, message, or functionality receive protection regardless of commercial impact on derivative markets.

Goldstein's work on moral rights examines tensions between authors' personal connections to their works and commercial realities requiring adaptation, translation, and modification. The Visual Artists Rights Act provides limited attribution and integrity protections for visual art, but excludes literary, musical, and dramatic works where similar dignitary interests exist. Goldstein advocates for expanded moral rights coverage combined with practical limitations ensuring these rights do not become weapons against legitimate artistic reinterpretation or commercial necessity. His framework influenced European debates on droit d'auteur reform and U.S. proposals for broader attribution requirements.

At 70+, Goldstein remains focused on emerging challenges in AI-generated works and cross-border digital enforcement: when machine learning systems produce creative outputs without human authorship, does copyright protection serve any social function? He argues that purely AI-generated works should remain in public domain since no human incentive is needed, though human-AI collaborative works may warrant protection for the human creative contributions. Similarly, his work on blockchain-based rights management examines how distributed ledger technology could streamline licensing while potentially creating new forms of technological lock-in requiring legal intervention. Through four decades of scholarship, his message remains consistent: copyright succeeds when it precisely targets incentives needed for creative production without expanding into perpetual control over information and ideas.
