# Pamela Samuelson

Pamela Samuelson is a leading copyright scholar whose work has shaped understanding of how fair use doctrine applies in digital environments, particularly for mass digitization, text mining, and software licensing. As Professor at University of California Berkeley School of Law and Co-Founder of the Berkeley Center for Law and Technology, she combines deep historical knowledge with technical sophistication to argue for copyright frameworks that serve constitutional purposes of promoting progress rather than creating perpetual property rights. Her central insight challenges the assumption that broader copyright protection always benefits creators: when exclusive rights expand beyond what is necessary to incentivize creation, they stifle the cumulative innovation and creative building upon existing works that drives cultural progress.

Her analysis of the Google Books project demonstrated that mass digitization constitutes transformative fair use because it enables search and discovery functionality fundamentally different from reading the original books. When Google scanned millions of library volumes to create searchable databases, it did not replace the market for books but rather enhanced their discoverability, benefiting authors and publishers while serving public interest in access to knowledge. Samuelson's scholarship influenced the Second Circuit's decision in Authors Guild v. Google finding fair use, though she critiqued the subsequent settlement attempt as overreaching by creating exclusive licensing arrangements that would have given Google control over orphan works without legislative authorization.

On software licensing, Samuelson examines how shrinkwrap and clickwrap agreements routinely restrict user rights that copyright law preserves through fair use, first sale, and reverse engineering exceptions. When license terms prohibit decompilation for interoperability purposes or prevent format shifting for accessibility, they override statutory privileges through private ordering. Samuelson advocates for legislative protections ensuring that users retain these essential rights regardless of contractual fine print, similar to how consumer protection law prevents waiver of fundamental rights in other contexts. Her analysis influenced European directives requiring enforceable fair use equivalents and U.S. proposals for balanced digital contracting rules.

Samuelson's work on open access publishing demonstrates how publicly funded research should be publicly accessible rather than locked behind publisher paywalls. Federal agencies' adoption of public access mandates ensures that taxpayer investments in scientific discovery benefit society through widespread dissemination while maintaining quality through established peer review processes. She critiques predatory publishing models that exploit academic labor for profit while restricting access to the very research they publish, arguing that sustainable scholarly communication requires institutional repositories and diamond open access journals supported by universities and funding agencies.

At 75+, Samuelson remains focused on emerging challenges in AI and machine learning: when large language models train on billions of copyrighted works, does this constitute fair use or does it require licensing? She argues that training for purposes of pattern recognition and statistical analysis is transformative because it extracts facts and relationships rather than copying expressive content, similar to how text mining was validated in earlier cases. However, she acknowledges that outputs reproducing substantial portions of training data may raise different questions requiring careful case-by-case analysis. Through four decades of scholarship, her message remains consistent: copyright law succeeds when it precisely targets incentives needed for creative production without creating broader monopolies that impede the very progress the Constitution intended to promote.
