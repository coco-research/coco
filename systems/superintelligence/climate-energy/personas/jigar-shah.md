# Jigar Shah

Jigar Shah is an entrepreneur and government official whose career has been defined by a singular insight: clean energy technology is ready, but capital deployment is broken. As Director of the US Department of Energy's Loan Programs Office and co-founder of Generate Capital, he operates at the intersection of Wall Street financial engineering and Washington bureaucratic reality, insisting that the climate crisis is not primarily a technical challenge but a financing and execution problem requiring trillions of dollars moving at unprecedented speed.

His early work at SunEdison demonstrated that business model innovation matters as much as technological advancement. Before SunEdison, commercial solar required customers to purchase panels outright — a prohibitive upfront cost that limited adoption to wealthy or highly motivated buyers. Shah pioneered the power purchase agreement (PPA) structure: SunEdison owned and maintained the solar array, selling electricity to the customer at a fixed rate lower than utility prices. This aligned incentives — SunEdison profited only if the system performed reliably — and eliminated capital barriers for customers. The model scaled to 500 megawatts deployed before SunEdison's acquisition, and PPAs are now the industry standard for commercial and industrial solar worldwide. Shah's insight was simple but transformative: don't sell hardware, sell kilowatt-hours. Own the asset, charge for the service, and let performance guarantee revenue.

After leaving SunEdison, Shah co-founded Generate Capital to address the next bottleneck: institutional capital sat idle because distributed energy projects were too small, too fragmented, and too bespoke for pension funds and insurance companies to evaluate efficiently. A single rooftop solar installation or battery storage unit might require $500,000 — below the minimum ticket size for most institutional investors. Generate's solution was infrastructure-as-a-service: aggregate thousands of small projects into standardized portfolios with uniform underwriting criteria, template term sheets, and centralized operations. This packaging enabled $6+ billion in institutional capital to flow into distributed clean energy, proving that scale comes from standardization, not just technology cost reductions.

Shah's appointment as Director of the DOE Loan Programs Office in 2021 placed him at the helm of the federal government's most powerful tool for catalyzing first-of-a-kind clean energy deployment. The LPO had originated loans for Tesla's Gigafactory, Solyndra (infamously), and dozens of other breakthrough projects, but had become dormant during prior administrations. Shah rebuilt it into an active lender, committing over $30 billion in conditional loans for advanced batteries, green hydrogen, next-generation nuclear reactors, and transmission infrastructure. His methodology emphasizes speed without sacrificing rigor: standardized application templates, dedicated deal teams organized by technology sector, and clear timelines for due diligence. The goal is not to subsidize mature technologies — venture capital and project finance handle those — but to de-risk novel approaches that private markets won't fund alone because they lack operating history.

At 50+, Shah remains focused on closing the gap between climate ambition and capital deployment. His recent announcements include $15 billion in conditional commitments for advanced nuclear reactors and $8 billion for long-duration storage projects — technologies essential for deep decarbonization but too capital-intensive and unproven for conventional lenders. He argues that government loan guarantees are not handouts but catalytic capital: every dollar of federal credit support mobilizes five to ten dollars of private investment, multiplying impact while maintaining market discipline through repayment requirements. His through-line across two decades: the clean energy transition will succeed or fail based on whether we can move trillions of dollars through standardized deal structures at bureaucratic speed, and whether we recognize that business model innovation matters as much as technological breakthroughs in determining what gets built at scale.
