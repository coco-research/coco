# Sarah Tishkoff

Sarah Tishkoff is a population geneticist whose work has fundamentally reshaped how we understand human genetic diversity, evolution, and the implications for precision medicine. Her research focuses on African populations — which contain more genetic variation than all other continents combined — and demonstrates that ignoring this diversity produces genomic medicine that works for some populations and fails for others.

Her landmark studies on lactase persistence showed that the ability to digest milk as adults evolved independently at least three separate times in Africa, driven by the cultural practice of pastoralism. This finding became a textbook example of gene-culture coevolution: a behavioral innovation (cattle herding) created selective pressure for a genetic adaptation (lactase persistence), which then spread rapidly through populations that adopted the practice. The work demonstrated that natural selection in humans is not a relic of the distant past but an ongoing force shaped by cultural choices.

Tishkoff's analysis of 121 African populations revealed fine-scale genetic structure invisible to previous studies, refining models of human origins, migration routes, and admixture events. Her work showed that African Americans carry genomic signatures from specific West African regions, enabling more precise ancestry inference and challenging simplistic notions of racial categories in genetics.

Her most consequential recent work addresses the equity crisis in precision medicine. By demonstrating that pharmacogenomic variants — genetic differences affecting drug metabolism and response — vary dramatically across African populations, she has shown that Eurocentric reference panels miss clinically actionable variation. A drug dose calibrated on European-ancestry patients can be toxic or ineffective for someone with different metabolic alleles. Her solution is not just technical but institutional: she co-leads the H3Africa Consortium, which builds genomic research capacity within Africa, trains African scientists, establishes biobanks governed by local communities, and ensures that African genomes are studied by African researchers rather than extracted for external benefit.

At 60+, Tishkoff remains active in the field — literally, collecting samples with community consent across remote regions — and in the lab, developing computational methods to detect natural selection and build ancestry-specific reference panels. Her recent publications include a pan-African pharmacogenomic resource covering 50+ ethnic groups, enabling clinicians to tailor drug dosing based on patient ancestry rather than applying one-size-fits-all guidelines derived from European cohorts. Her through-line across three decades: genomic science must serve the populations it studies, and precision medicine that excludes the majority of human genetic diversity is neither precise nor just.
