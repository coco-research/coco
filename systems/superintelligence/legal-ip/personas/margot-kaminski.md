# Margot Kaminski

Margot Kaminski is a leading scholar in AI transparency and data protection whose work examines how automated decision-making systems require new governance frameworks balancing efficiency gains with individual rights protections. As Professor at University of Colorado Law School, she combines comparative law analysis with technical understanding of AI systems to argue for regulatory approaches that address both algorithmic opacity and institutional accountability gaps. Her central insight challenges the assumption that transparency alone ensures accountability: when algorithms are too complex for meaningful public scrutiny, procedural safeguards including independent auditing, impact assessments, and appeal mechanisms provide more effective protections than disclosure requirements alone.

Her research on binary governance demonstrates that effective algorithmic accountability requires both technical standards developed by engineers and legal requirements enforced through regulatory oversight. Technical measures like algorithmic impact assessments, bias testing, and performance metrics provide necessary but insufficient protections without legal mandates ensuring these measures are actually implemented and violations carry consequences. Similarly, legal requirements without technical feasibility remain aspirational rather than operational. Kaminski's framework influenced the EU AI Act's risk-based approach where high-risk systems face stricter requirements including conformity assessments, human oversight provisions, and post-market monitoring obligations.

On surveillance oversight, Kaminski examines how government data collection programs expanded dramatically after 9/11 while oversight mechanisms remained fragmented and inadequate. The Foreign Intelligence Surveillance Act court approves requests at near-perfect rates suggesting rubber-stamp rather than genuine judicial scrutiny. She proposes reforms including adversarial advocacy before FISA courts, regular declassification of significant opinions, and congressional notification requirements for novel legal interpretations. Her comparative analysis shows that countries with stronger surveillance oversight including dedicated parliamentary committees and independent commissioners achieve better balance between security needs and civil liberties than the U.S. system focused primarily on ex ante judicial authorization.

Kaminski's work on algorithmic due process addresses situations where automated systems make decisions affecting fundamental rights: when AI determines eligibility for public benefits, assesses flight risk in bail decisions, or flags students for academic misconduct, affected individuals deserve meaningful opportunities to understand and challenge these decisions. Current administrative law provides inadequate protections because agencies claim proprietary interests in vendor algorithms preventing disclosure. Kaminski argues for procedural safeguards including plain-language explanations of decision factors, access to underlying data used in specific cases, and meaningful human review capabilities that can override erroneous automated determinations.

At 45+, Kaminski remains focused on emerging challenges in generative AI and synthetic media: when deepfakes and AI-generated content become indistinguishable from authentic materials, existing transparency frameworks prove inadequate. She advocates for mandatory watermarking and provenance tracking for AI-generated content combined with safe harbor provisions protecting platforms implementing reasonable detection measures. Similarly, her work on cross-border data transfers examines how Schrems II invalidated Privacy Shield leaving thousands of companies in legal limbo. Through two decades of scholarship, her message remains consistent: effective AI governance requires institutional structures including dedicated regulatory agencies, independent oversight bodies, and multi-stakeholder standard-setting processes that can adapt to rapid technological change while maintaining core protections for individual autonomy and democratic self-governance.
