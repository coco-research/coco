# Feng Zhang

Feng Zhang is a genome engineer whose discoveries have repeatedly expanded the CRISPR toolbox beyond the original Cas9 system. As Core Institute Member at the Broad Institute and Professor at MIT, he has identified and characterized dozens of novel CRISPR-Cas enzymes from bacterial genomes, each with distinct properties — smaller size for viral delivery, RNA targeting for reversible modulation, or precision editing without DNA cleavage. His work transformed CRISPR from a single molecular scissors into a diverse platform of programmable nucleic acid manipulators.

His 2013 demonstration that CRISPR-Cas9 functions in human cells was the inflection point that launched the therapeutic genome editing field. Within months, labs worldwide were adapting the system for disease modeling, functional genomics, and potential cures. But Zhang's most consequential contributions came after the initial breakthrough. His development of base editors — enzymes that chemically convert one DNA letter to another without cutting the double helix — eliminated the error-prone repair outcomes that plagued early CRISPR applications. Prime editing, published in 2019, went further: a search-and-replace mechanism capable of precise insertions, deletions, and all twelve possible base conversions, covering up to 89% of known disease-causing variants without generating double-strand breaks.

Zhang's exploration of RNA-targeting Cas13 systems opened a parallel therapeutic avenue. Unlike DNA editors that make permanent changes, Cas13 degrades specific RNA transcripts reversibly — ideal for diseases where transient modulation is safer than permanent alteration. The same enzyme enabled SHERLOCK, a paper-strip diagnostic that detects viral RNA with attomolar sensitivity, deployed during Ebola and COVID-19 outbreaks in resource-limited settings.

His current focus addresses the delivery bottleneck that constrains all genome medicines. Even the most precise editor is useless if it cannot reach the target cells in a living patient. Zhang's lab engineers ultra-compact Cas variants small enough to fit inside adeno-associated virus (AAV) vectors alongside regulatory elements and guide RNAs — the only clinically approved delivery vehicle for systemic administration. His recent publications demonstrate dual-editor AAV payloads achieving therapeutic correction of sickle cell disease mutations in mouse models, a milestone toward in vivo cures that bypass the complexity and cost of ex vivo cell therapies. At 43, Zhang remains the most prolific expander of the genome engineering toolkit, insisting that nature's diversity of CRISPR systems holds solutions we have barely begun to explore.
