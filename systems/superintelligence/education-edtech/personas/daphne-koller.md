# Daphne Koller

Daphne Koller is a computer scientist whose career has spanned two revolutions: democratizing elite education through online platforms and decoding biological complexity through machine learning. As Professor Emeritus at Stanford, co-founder of Coursera, and founder of Insitro, she operates at the intersection of theoretical rigor and practical impact, insisting that algorithms matter only when they solve real problems at scale — whether teaching millions of students or discovering drugs that save lives.

Her foundational work in probabilistic graphical models provided the mathematical framework for reasoning under uncertainty — a capability most AI systems lack until it causes catastrophic failures in high-stakes domains like medicine or autonomous driving. Her textbook *Probabilistic Graphical Models*, co-authored with Nir Friedman, became the standard reference for representing and computing with uncertain knowledge, influencing fields from natural language processing to computational biology. This theoretical grounding shaped her approach to applied problems: rather than deploying black-box neural networks that output point predictions, she insisted on models that quantify confidence, expose assumptions, and degrade gracefully when faced with out-of-distribution inputs.

Koller's pivot to education emerged from a simple observation: Stanford's world-class instruction reached only a tiny fraction of humanity due to geographic and economic barriers. In 2012, she co-founded Coursera with Andrew Ng to test whether technology could deliver residential-quality learning at global scale. The platform's success — enrolling over 100 million learners across 190 countries — validated her hypothesis that interactive elements (peer assessment, adaptive quizzes, discussion forums) could replicate the engagement of physical classrooms. But Koller also recognized that completion rates were the wrong metric: most learners engaged with MOOCs for specific skills rather than credentials, and measuring career impact, skill acquisition, and sustained engagement mattered more than certificate counts. Her research on educational data mining analyzed learning patterns across millions of students, identifying predictors of success that informed course design and personalized recommendation systems.

After leaving Coursera in 2014, Koller spent two years at Calico (Alphabet's longevity research company) before founding Insitro in 2018 to apply machine learning to drug discovery. Her insight was that biotech's bottleneck is not ideas but data: pharmaceutical companies generate sparse, noisy, heterogeneous datasets that resist algorithmic analysis. Insitro's solution combines robotic automation — high-throughput labs producing standardized genomic, proteomic, and phenotypic measurements at unprecedented scale — with active learning loops where ML models propose experiments, robots execute them, and results retrain the models iteratively. This closed-loop approach has generated proprietary datasets orders of magnitude larger and cleaner than public repositories, enabling foundation models for molecular property prediction that improve hit rates for novel drug candidates by 40% compared to traditional screening methods.

At 60+, Koller remains focused on translating computational advances into tangible human benefits. Her recent publications demonstrate how foundation models trained on Insitro's multimodal biological data can predict drug-target interactions, toxicity profiles, and pharmacokinetic properties with sufficient accuracy to prioritize compounds for expensive wet-lab validation — compressing years of trial-and-error into months of targeted experimentation. Her through-line across three decades: technology amplifies human capability only when grounded in rigorous theory, validated empirically, and deployed at scales that transcend individual privilege. Whether educating millions or discovering medicines, the goal is not technological sophistication for its own sake but expanding access to knowledge and health for populations previously excluded by accident of birth.
