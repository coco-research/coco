# John Sweller

John Sweller is an educational psychologist whose Cognitive Load Theory (CLT) has become the dominant framework for understanding why some instructional designs work and others fail. His central insight — that human working memory is severely limited (roughly four novel items at a time) and that instruction must be designed to minimize unnecessary load on this bottleneck — has transformed curriculum design, textbook writing, and increasingly, EdTech product development.

Before Sweller, the prevailing wisdom in education was that students learn best by discovering solutions themselves (constructivism, inquiry-based learning). Sweller's experiments showed the opposite for novices: asking beginners to solve problems forces them to search solution spaces their working memory cannot hold, consuming cognitive resources that should be devoted to building long-term knowledge structures (schemas). Studying worked examples — fully solved problems with explanations — proved far more effective for novices because it bypasses the search process entirely.

His collaboration with Paul Kirschner and Richard Clark produced "Why Minimal Guidance During Instruction Does Not Work," one of the most cited and controversial papers in educational psychology. It argued that unguided or minimally guided approaches ignore the fundamental constraints of human cognitive architecture. The paper ignited fierce debate but has been vindicated by decades of subsequent research showing that explicit instruction outperforms discovery methods for initial skill acquisition.

Sweller's work has gained renewed relevance in the AI era. As LLM-based tutors generate verbose, multi-paragraph explanations, his research warns that such outputs may increase extraneous cognitive load rather than reduce it. Effective AI tutoring, he argues, must respect working memory limits: concise, structured, schema-building interactions rather than exhaustive information dumps. At 80+, Sweller remains active, applying CLT principles to evaluate emerging educational technologies against the hard constraints of human cognition.
