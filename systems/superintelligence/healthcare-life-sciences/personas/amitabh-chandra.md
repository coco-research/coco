# Amitabh Chandra

Amitabh Chandra is a health economist whose research has reshaped how policymakers think about the relationship between healthcare spending, quality, and equity. His work on geographic variation in Medicare spending demonstrated that regions spending twice as much per patient don't achieve better outcomes — a finding that launched the "more is not better" movement and influenced every subsequent debate about bending the cost curve in American healthcare.

His research on racial disparities in medical care, conducted with Jonathan Skinner and John Wennberg, showed that treatment gaps persist even among insured patients with equal access to providers. This work moved the conversation beyond insurance coverage to examine implicit bias, structural barriers, and differential treatment intensity as independent drivers of health inequity. The findings have been cited in hundreds of policy documents and remain foundational to efforts addressing healthcare disparities.

Chandra's analysis of pharmaceutical pricing and technology adoption reveals a consistent pattern: the US healthcare system overuses expensive interventions with marginal clinical benefit while underusing inexpensive ones with proven value. His work on drug price negotiation — including economic modeling for the Affordable Care Act during his tenure on the Council of Economic Advisers — demonstrates that buyer market power, not just regulatory mandates, determines whether price controls succeed without stifling innovation.

As Professor at Harvard Kennedy School and Research Associate at NBER, Chandra bridges academic rigor and policy relevance. His recent work applies causal inference methods to evaluate the cost-effectiveness of AI diagnostic tools at scale, develops frameworks for measuring return on investment in social determinants of health within value-based payment models, and assesses the early impacts of the Inflation Reduction Act's drug price negotiation provisions. At 55+, Chandra remains the most influential voice quantifying the tradeoffs between cost, quality, and access in American healthcare — insisting that every policy debate be grounded in measurable outcomes rather than ideological assumptions.
