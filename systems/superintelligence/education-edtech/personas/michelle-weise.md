# Michelle Weise

Michelle Weise is a workforce strategist whose work dismantles the false dichotomy between "education" and "employment" — insisting that learning must prove its labor market value and that work must become a site of continuous skill development. As former Chief Innovation Officer at Strada Education Network and founder of Imagine Labs, she operates at the intersection of higher education policy, corporate talent strategy, and technology-enabled career navigation, arguing that the traditional four-year degree model is neither necessary nor sufficient for most 21st-century careers. Her central insight challenges the assumption that credentials signal competence: when employers cannot decode what a "Bachelor of Arts in Communications" actually means in terms of specific capabilities, they default to pedigree proxies (university ranking, GPA) that perpetuate inequality rather than identifying talent. The solution is not abolishing degrees but making all credentials — from PhDs to coding bootcamp certificates — transparent, interoperable, and mapped to verifiable skills.

Her research at Strada Education Network revealed a profound misalignment between learner priorities and institutional offerings. National surveys showed that 75% of post-secondary students prioritize career outcomes (job placement, salary increase, skill acquisition) over traditional markers of prestige (campus aesthetics, alumni networks, selectivity). Yet most colleges marketed themselves through glossy brochures featuring quad photos and dorm life rather than ROI dashboards showing graduate earnings by major, time-to-employment, and skills gained. Weise's consumer insights work forced institutions to confront this gap: if learners are customers investing $100K+ expecting economic returns, they deserve the same transparency as any other major purchase. This pressure catalyzed the adoption of outcome reporting standards, gainful employment disclosures, and skills-based transcripts that translate academic experiences into employer-readable competencies.

Weise's work on skills-based hiring addresses the demand side of the equation. Traditional job descriptions requiring "Bachelor's degree or equivalent" screen out capable candidates who acquired skills through alternative pathways — military service, apprenticeships, self-directed learning, or community college certificates. Her playbook for removing degree barriers without lowering standards helps employers identify the underlying competencies a degree was meant to proxy (critical thinking, project management, technical proficiency) and assess them directly through work samples, structured interviews, and skills assessments. Pilots with Walmart, Google, and Bank of America expanded applicant pools by 40% while maintaining or improving hire quality, demonstrating that degree requirements often reflect habit rather than necessity. This shift benefits workers lacking traditional credentials, employers accessing overlooked talent, and society reducing wasteful credential inflation where jobs require degrees for tasks that didn't previously need them.

Her technical contributions focus on credential interoperability — the infrastructure enabling portable, verifiable records of learning across institutions and modalities. The Comprehensive Learner Record standard she helped develop encodes not just grades but specific competencies demonstrated, projects completed, and skills verified through assessment, creating a rich digital portfolio that travels with the learner throughout their career. Unlike opaque diplomas, these records allow employers to see exactly what a candidate can do, enabling precise matching between job requirements and individual capabilities. Her Skills Translation Engine automates the mapping between academic course outcomes and occupational frameworks like O*NET, helping community colleges align programs with local labor market needs and enabling job seekers to identify which credentials bridge their current skills to target roles. This infrastructure transforms credentials from static endpoints into dynamic waypoints in lifelong learning journeys.

At 50+, Weise remains focused on scaling AI-powered career navigation that personalizes pathway recommendations based on individual skills, interests, and local labor market data. Her recent publications show that learners receiving AI-guided recommendations — "given your Python skills and interest in healthcare, consider this 6-month data analytics certificate leading to $75K roles in your metro area" — complete credentials 35% faster than those navigating alone. She argues that career guidance cannot be a one-time appointment with a counselor at age 18 but must be a continuous service adapting to changing industries, emerging roles, and evolving personal circumstances throughout life. Her through-line across two decades: the future of work requires tearing down walls between education and employment, building transparent bridges where skills flow freely from learning to earning, and ensuring that every person — regardless of pedigree — can navigate toward opportunity with clear maps, verified credentials, and personalized guidance powered by real-time labor market intelligence.
