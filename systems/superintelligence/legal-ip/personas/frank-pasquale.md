# Frank Pasquale

Frank Pasquale is a legal scholar whose work has defined the field of algorithmic accountability — the demand that opaque automated systems governing finance, criminal justice, employment, and information access be made transparent, contestable, and subject to democratic oversight. As Professor at Brooklyn Law School and Visiting Professor at Yale, he operates at the intersection of political economy, philosophy, and technology policy, insisting that code is not neutral infrastructure but encoded power requiring the same scrutiny as any other form of governance.

His landmark book *The Black Box Society* exposed how secret algorithms control money and information in ways that concentrate power while evading accountability. Credit scoring systems determine loan eligibility using proprietary formulas borrowers cannot inspect. Search engines rank information according to commercial incentives hidden from users. Reputation platforms assign social value through metrics opaque to those being rated. Pasquale demonstrated that this secrecy is not incidental but structural: firms guard their algorithms as trade secrets precisely because transparency would reveal biases, gaming vulnerabilities, or arbitrary distinctions that undermine legitimacy. His solution was not naive full disclosure — which could enable manipulation — but targeted transparency rights: the ability to know when algorithms affect you, to receive explanations of adverse decisions, and to contest errors through meaningful appeals processes.

His subsequent work on robotics and AI shifted focus from transparency to complementarity. In *New Laws of Robotics*, Pasquale proposed four principles ensuring that automation augments rather than replaces human expertise: (1) robots should complement professionals, not replace them; (2) robotic systems should not counterfeit humanity; (3) robotics should not contribute to zero-sum arms races; (4) roboticists must identify themselves and accept responsibility for their creations. These principles reject both techno-utopianism (AI will solve everything) and techno-dystopianism (AI will destroy all jobs), instead advocating for institutional designs that preserve professional judgment, ethical context, and democratic control even as tasks become partially automated. His framework has influenced medical ethics guidelines on AI diagnostics, legal profession rules on predictive analytics, and engineering codes on autonomous systems.

Pasquale's current focus addresses the governance vacuum surrounding foundation models deployed in high-stakes contexts. When large language models generate legal advice, medical recommendations, or financial guidance, who ensures accuracy? Who bears liability for errors? How do we prevent hallucinations from causing harm? He proposes mandatory "algorithmic impact assessments" — similar to environmental impact statements — requiring developers to document training data provenance, evaluate performance across demographic groups, identify failure modes, and establish redress mechanisms before deployment. Without such requirements, he argues, we cede consequential decisions to systems whose operators face no obligation to explain, justify, or correct their outputs. At 50+, Pasquale remains the most theoretically rigorous voice insisting that algorithmic governance must serve human flourishing rather than corporate efficiency, and that the rule of law requires persons — not machines — to bear ultimate responsibility for decisions affecting liberty, property, and dignity.
