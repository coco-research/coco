# Nina Tandon

Nina Tandon is a tissue engineer whose work transforms the ancient dream of growing replacement body parts into clinical reality. As co-founder and CEO of EpiBone and Adjunct Professor at Columbia University, she operates at the intersection of electrical engineering, cell biology, and regenerative medicine, insisting that the human body already possesses the machinery to heal itself — we just need to provide the right physical and chemical cues to activate it. Her central insight challenges the transplant paradigm dominating organ replacement: instead of harvesting tissue from donors (creating shortages and rejection risks) or implanting synthetic materials (lacking biological integration), we can grow patient-specific organs from their own cells in laboratory bioreactors, eliminating immunosuppression, donor site morbidity, and ethical compromises while producing living tissue that remodels and adapts like native bone.

Her technical innovation centers on electrical stimulation as a language cells understand. Bone tissue is piezoelectric — it generates electrical fields when mechanically stressed, and conversely, grows toward applied electrical gradients. Tandon's research demonstrated that carefully tuned voltage patterns accelerate osteogenic differentiation of mesenchymal stem cells, improve mineral deposition, and enhance mechanical strength of engineered constructs beyond what biochemical factors alone achieve. This insight shaped EpiBone's proprietary bioreactor design: patient-derived stem cells seeded onto biomimetic scaffolds receive precisely controlled electrical stimulation mimicking the natural signals bone experiences during healing, guiding them to form organized, load-bearing tissue rather than disorganized callus. The result is autologous bone grafts genetically identical to the patient, vascularized and innervated, capable of integrating seamlessly with surrounding skeleton without foreign body response or rejection.

EpiBone's clinical translation validates this approach in humans. Traditional bone grafting for spinal fusion or craniofacial reconstruction requires harvesting bone from the patient's hip (causing chronic pain, infection risk, and limited supply) or using cadaveric allografts (carrying disease transmission risk and slower integration). EpiBone's process takes a small blood draw or bone marrow aspirate, expands the patient's stem cells in culture, seeds them onto a scaffold shaped to the exact defect geometry via CT imaging, and cultivates the construct in a bioreactor for several weeks until it achieves sufficient density and maturity for implantation. Phase II trial results show 95% fusion rates in lumbar spinal surgery — matching or exceeding gold-standard autograft outcomes — without any donor site complications. Patients report dramatically reduced postoperative pain and faster return to function, validating the hypothesis that eliminating the second surgical site improves recovery independent of the graft's biological performance.

Her work on biomanufacturing scale-up addresses the bottleneck constraining all regenerative therapies: growing tissue in a academic lab differs fundamentally from producing it consistently at clinical scale. Academic protocols rely on manual handling, static culture conditions, and small batch sizes unsuitable for commercial viability. Tandon's patented perfusion bioreactor systems automate nutrient delivery, waste removal, and electrical stimulation across hundreds of constructs simultaneously, maintaining uniform microenvironments essential for reproducible tissue quality. Closed-loop monitoring adjusts parameters in real-time based on metabolic markers and impedance measurements, reducing batch failures and operator variability. This engineering rigor transforms tissue engineering from artisanal craft to industrial process, enabling EpiBone to manufacture hundreds of patient-specific grafts annually with regulatory-grade documentation satisfying FDA requirements for consistency, sterility, and potency.

At 45+, Tandon remains focused on expanding autologous regeneration beyond bone to other skeletal tissues — cartilage for joint repair, tendon for rotator cuff reconstruction, cranial plates for trauma — while advocating for regulatory frameworks recognizing living implants as distinct from drugs or devices. Her recent publications analyze how AI-driven bioreactor control systems can optimize stimulation protocols for individual patients based on their cells' real-time responses, personalizing not just the graft geometry but the cultivation recipe itself. She argues that regenerative medicine's promise lies not in replacing biology with technology but in amplifying biology's native capacities through engineering precision — conducting the orchestra of cellular processes rather than composing new music from scratch. Her through-line across two decades: the future of medicine is not better prosthetics or smarter drugs but activated biology, where every patient carries the raw materials for their own cure and engineers provide the scaffolds, signals, and systems to unlock it.
