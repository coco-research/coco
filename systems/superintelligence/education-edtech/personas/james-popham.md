# W. James Popham

W. James Popham (1930–2020) was an educational measurement specialist whose work fundamentally reshaped how America tests students — shifting from norm-referenced comparisons ranking students against each other to criterion-referenced assessments measuring whether students can perform specific skills or demonstrate defined knowledge. As Professor Emeritus at UCLA and founder of IOX Assessment Associates, he operated at the intersection of psychometric theory and classroom practice, insisting that tests must measure what they claim to measure and that assessment results are only useful when aligned with instruction. His central insight challenges the assumption that standardized tests are neutral arbiters of merit: when tests sample content disconnected from what teachers actually teach, they measure opportunity gaps rather than ability, producing invalid scores that misinform parents, misguide policymakers, and demoralize educators forced to choose between teaching the curriculum or teaching to the test.

His 1971 paper launching the criterion-referenced testing movement drew a sharp distinction that had been blurred in practice: norm-referenced tests ask "how does this student compare to others?" by design ensuring a bell curve distribution where half always fall below average regardless of absolute performance, while criterion-referenced tests ask "can the student do X?" against an external standard where all students could theoretically master the objective or none could. This seemingly technical distinction carries profound implications for instruction. When a fourth-grade math test is norm-referenced, improving teaching so all students learn fractions well creates a statistical impossibility — the test must still rank some as failures. When the same test is criterion-referenced to a specific standard like "adds fractions with unlike denominators," universal mastery becomes the goal, and the test serves its proper function: diagnosing who needs more support and verifying that instruction worked. Popham demonstrated that criterion-referenced interpretations yield more actionable diagnostic information because they pinpoint exactly which objectives students have or haven't achieved, enabling targeted remediation rather than vague labels like "below basic."

Popham's influence extended from theory into the machinery of American schooling through IOX Assessment Associates, the consultancy he founded to help states design accountability systems. During the 1970s and 1980s, as states moved from voluntary testing to mandatory minimum competency programs, IOX designed criterion-referenced assessments for California, Florida, and over twenty other jurisdictions. These tests operationalized Popham's principles: clear domain specifications defining exactly what knowledge and skills would be measured, item blueprints ensuring proportional representation of content areas, and standard-setting procedures establishing defensible cut scores separating proficient from non-proficient performance. His methodology emphasized content validity above all — the degree to which test items actually sample from the taught curriculum rather than generic standards or commercially available test banks. When tests lack content validity, he warned, they incentivize teaching to the test rather than teaching for understanding, as rational teachers abandon untested curriculum portions to drill tested items. This prediction proved prescient during No Child Left Behind's high-stakes regime, where narrow tested subjects crowded out arts, social studies, and physical education precisely because those areas carried no accountability weight.

His textbooks translated psychometric jargon into teacher-accessible language, making concepts like reliability, validity, and item discrimination comprehensible to practitioners without statistics training. *Classroom Assessment: What Teachers Need to Know* became the standard text in virtually every US teacher preparation program, not because it dumbed down measurement theory but because it connected abstract principles to daily decisions: How do I write a multiple-choice item that distinguishes students who understand from those who guess? How many test items do I need for reliable grades? When should I use essay questions versus selected-response formats? Popham insisted that assessment literacy is not optional for teachers — if you cannot distinguish reliability (consistency of scores across administrations) from validity (accuracy of score interpretations for intended uses), you cannot responsibly assign grades, diagnose learning needs, or communicate achievement to parents. A test reliable enough for grouping students may lack validity for evaluating teaching quality; a valid measure of factual recall may misrepresent critical thinking capacity. These distinctions matter enormously when test scores determine funding allocations, teacher evaluations, and school closures.

Though Popham died in 2020, his framework persists in every state accountability system using criterion-referenced assessments aligned to academic standards. His warnings about validity threats in high-stakes contexts remain urgent as testing expands into teacher evaluation, school grading, and college admissions. His recent relevance extends to AI-generated assessments: adaptive algorithms promising personalized testing must still satisfy content validity requirements — sampling representatively from the curriculum, avoiding construct-irrelevant variance from interface design or cultural bias, and producing scores interpretable for their intended purposes. At his death, Popham left behind not just technical manuals but a moral imperative: assessment is too powerful to be left to psychometricians alone, and teachers must understand measurement principles well enough to demand tests that measure what matters rather than merely what is easy to score.
