# Paul Kirschner

Paul Kirschner is an educational psychologist who has spent his career testing popular teaching methods against the hard evidence of cognitive science — and finding most of them wanting. His collaboration with John Sweller and Richard Clark on "Why Minimal Guidance During Instruction Does Not Work" became one of the most influential and controversial papers in educational psychology, demonstrating that discovery learning, inquiry-based teaching, and other minimally guided approaches ignore the fundamental constraints of human working memory.

Kirschner's particular contribution is his willingness to name and dismantle educational sacred cows. His book *Urban Legends About Learning and Education* systematically debunks myths that persist despite decades of disconfirming evidence: learning styles (no empirical support), digital natives (young people use technology fluently but don't know how to learn with it), left-brain/right-brain teaching (neuromyth), and the idea that students learn better when taught in their "preferred" modality. Each legend is confronted with meta-analyses showing zero or negative effects.

His work on collaborative learning adds nuance to the cognitive load framework: group work can enhance learning, but only when designed with individual accountability, clear task structures, and mechanisms to prevent social loafing. Without these safeguards, collaboration becomes a cognitive load multiplier rather than a reducer — students spend working memory coordinating the group rather than building schemas.

In the AI era, Kirschner's warnings have gained urgency. He argues that LLM-based tutors and "personalized learning" algorithms often increase extraneous cognitive load through verbose, unstructured explanations that lack the worked-example scaffolding novices need. Effective AI tutoring, he insists, must respect cognitive architecture: concise, structured, schema-building interactions rather than exhaustive information dumps tailored to mythical "learning styles." At 70+, Kirschner remains active, applying cognitive load principles to evaluate emerging educational technologies against the evidence of how humans actually learn.
