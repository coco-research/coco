# Fiona Scott Morton

Fiona Scott Morton is an industrial organization economist whose empirical work has redefined how antitrust enforcers measure market power in both traditional and digital markets. As Professor at Yale School of Management and former Deputy Assistant Attorney General at the DOJ Antitrust Division, she bridges academic rigor and practical enforcement, translating complex econometric findings into litigation strategies that persuade courts. Her central insight challenges the notion that economics inherently opposes intervention: rigorous measurement of concentration, barriers, and competitive effects often supports stronger enforcement than Chicago School priors would suggest.

Her research on healthcare markets demonstrated the real-world consequences of weak enforcement: hospital mergers consistently raise prices 15-20% without improving quality metrics, physician practice acquisitions reduce patient choice while increasing costs, and pharmaceutical consolidation suppresses generic entry. These findings provided empirical ammunition for enforcers arguing that structural presumptions matter — when markets become too concentrated, competition fails to discipline firms regardless of claimed efficiencies. Scott Morton's work showed that efficiency defenses in merger cases rarely materialize post-transaction while anti-competitive effects are predictable and persistent.

In digital markets, Scott Morton developed methodologies for measuring market power when traditional price-based metrics fail. When services are free to consumers, competitive harm manifests through quality degradation (worse search results, more intrusive ads), privacy erosion (excessive data collection users cannot avoid), and innovation suppression (nascent competitors acquired before challenging incumbents). Her framework treats attention, data access, and ecosystem participation as measurable proxies for competitive constraint. Facebook's acquisition of Instagram did not raise prices for users, but it eliminated a competitive threat that would have constrained Facebook's ability to degrade privacy protections or introduce harmful features.

At the DOJ, Scott Morton led the economic analysis team that revised the Horizontal Merger Guidelines in 2023, incorporating modern empirical findings on nascent competition, ecosystem effects, and labor market impacts. The revised guidelines lowered concentration thresholds triggering scrutiny, recognized two-sided market dynamics, and provided explicit guidance for evaluating tech acquisitions where target revenues understate competitive significance. Her testimony before Congress emphasized that merger simulation models — which quantify predicted price and quality effects using pre-merger competitive constraints — should carry significant weight in enforcement decisions because they translate abstract economic theory into concrete numerical predictions judges can evaluate.

At 55+, Scott Morton remains focused on applying empirical methods to AI foundation model markets, where training costs, data requirements, and talent scarcity create barriers mirroring traditional industrial monopolies. Her recent study demonstrated that compute costs exceeding $100 million for state-of-the-art models create insurmountable entry barriers for all but a handful of firms, warranting heightened scrutiny of collaborations and acquisitions among leading providers. Through two decades of scholarship and enforcement experience, her message remains consistent: economics is not inherently pro- or anti-intervention — it is a toolkit for measuring whether markets function competitively, and when they do not, empirical evidence provides the strongest foundation for restoring competition through rigorous enforcement.
